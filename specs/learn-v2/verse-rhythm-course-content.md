# Verse & rhythm: complete course content

48 lessons in 12 modules. Each lesson follows Understand → Cases → Depth → Reflection. Sources accessed 11 October 2026. This is a researched content prototype; rare metre families and Vedic traditions are introductions, not exhaustive qualifications.


## Module 1: Hear the building blocks

Count sounds, understand time and locate the parts of a verse.

### 1. A verse has more than one kind of rhythm

**Outcome:** Separate syllable count, weight and recitation.

Say a short line slowly. Some syllables take more room than others, but that does not mean they must be louder. Metre is a recurring arrangement of syllables or time units. A melody is one way to perform it.

We will first learn what the text prescribes, then return to how a reader gives the verse life.

**Cases and examples**

- **क · का | ka · kā** — Both have one vowel and therefore one syllable. The vowel in kā is long; the vowel in ka is short.
- **A spoken verse and a sung verse** — The tune may change while the underlying metrical pattern stays the same. Do not count musical notes as Sanskrit syllables.

**Go deeper**

Keep three layers separate: the sounds in the text, the metrical positions they occupy, and the performance. Our L/G notation describes metrical weight; it does not specify pitch, emotion or loudness. A mātrā is a relative unit for analysis, not a command to pronounce every syllable for a fixed number of milliseconds.

**Reflect:** Does a louder ka automatically become guru?

**Explanation:** No. Loudness is not a cause of metrical weight. Look at vowel length and the sounds that follow.

Sources: [Vṛttaratnākara · Definitions](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/Textual-Studies/Vrttaratnakara/1-Samjnabhidhana.htm); [Apte · Sanskrit Prosody (1890)](https://www.sanskrit-lexicon.uni-koeln.de/scans/csldev/csldoc/build/dictionaries/prefaces/ap90app1.html)

### 2. Find the vowel; find the syllable

**Outcome:** Count vowel nuclei rather than written shapes.

Each Sanskrit syllable has one vowel. A consonant cluster can look complicated and still belong with a single vowel. Read the sound sequence rather than counting Unicode characters, letters or visual blocks.

In the teaching divisions below, consonants usually stay with the following vowel. This is a traditional way of displaying syllables.

**Cases and examples**

- **योग → यो · ग | yo · ga** — Two vowels, two syllables. The dependent vowel sign in यो is not an extra syllable.
- **व्याकरण → व्या · क · र · ण | vyā · ka · ra · ṇa** — Four vowels, four syllables. व्य is a consonant combination, not two vowels.
- **क्षेत्र → क्षे · त्र | kṣe · tra** — Two syllables. क्ष contains k + ṣ; त्र contains t + r. Neither cluster creates an extra vowel.

**Go deeper**

A final consonant joins the preceding syllable: सुखम् → सु · खम्. Anusvāra and visarga also stay with their preceding vowel. You may see phonetic divisions such as dhar-ma elsewhere and traditional divisions such as dha-rma here. These place written consonants differently; they must give the same vowel count and weight analysis.

**Reflect:** How many syllables are in मित्रम् (mitram)?

**Explanation:** Two: mi-tram. The cluster tr does not create a vowel, and final m is not a separate syllable.

Sources: [LearnSanskrit.org · Syllables](https://www.learnsanskrit.org/guide/core/syllables/); [Vṛttaratnākara · Definitions](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/Textual-Studies/Vrttaratnakara/1-Samjnabhidhana.htm)

### 3. Vowel length without guessing

**Outcome:** Recognize the short and long vowel inventories.

Learn the vowel itself before judging the whole syllable. The ordinary short vowels are a, i, u, ṛ and ḷ. The long vowels are ā, ī, ū, ṝ, e, ai, o and au. E and o count as long in ordinary Classical Sanskrit metre even without a macron in transliteration.

**Cases and examples**

- **अ आ · इ ई · उ ऊ** — These pairs make the length contrast visible. Short and long are part of the word, not expressive choices.
- **ऋ and रि | ṛ and ri** — Ṛ is a vowel; r is a consonant in ri. Both examples have one vowel, but different sounds.
- **ए ऐ ओ औ | e ai o au** — Each is one long vowel nucleus. Ai and au are not two metrical syllables.

**Go deeper**

Ṝ is rare and ḷ is very rare; rarity does not remove them from the system. Pluta, an explicitly prolonged vowel often marked with 3, belongs to a separate discussion of phonetics and recitation. Do not turn every pluta into an ordinary third weight category in Classical L/G pattern matching.

**Reflect:** Is दे (de) light because e has no macron?

**Explanation:** No. E is a long vowel in ordinary Classical Sanskrit, so de is heavy.

Sources: [Apte · Sanskrit Prosody (1890)](https://www.sanskrit-lexicon.uni-koeln.de/scans/csldev/csldoc/build/dictionaries/prefaces/ap90app1.html); [Vṛttaratnākara · Definitions](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/Textual-Studies/Vrttaratnakara/1-Samjnabhidhana.htm)

### 4. From syllable to pāda to stanza

**Outcome:** Locate quarters before trying to identify a metre.

A pāda is a metrical division of a verse. Many Classical Sanskrit stanzas have four pādas. Editions often print them on two lines, so a printed line can contain two pādas. A line break caused by a small screen is not a metrical boundary.

**Cases and examples**

- **धर्मक्षेत्रे कुरुक्षेत्रे / समवेता युयुत्सवः** — The first two pādas of Bhagavadgītā 1.1. Each has eight syllables. Together they make the first half of the stanza.
- **मामकाः पाण्डवाश्चैव / किमकुर्वत सञ्जय** — The remaining two pādas. The question continues through the verse: what did my people and the Pāṇḍavas do?

**Go deeper**

The single daṇḍa । and double daṇḍa ॥ are useful editorial clues, but layout and punctuation are not enough to establish a metre. Vedic stanzas can have different numbers of pādas: Gāyatrī commonly has three. Start with the text and its tradition; do not force every poem into four equal quarters.

**Reflect:** A verse occupies four lines on a phone. Must it have four pādas?

**Explanation:** No. Screen wrapping has no metrical authority. Find the verse structure from its source and pattern.

Sources: [LearnSanskrit.org · Syllables](https://www.learnsanskrit.org/guide/core/syllables/); [Vṛttaratnākara · Definitions](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/Textual-Studies/Vrttaratnakara/1-Samjnabhidhana.htm); [Piṅgala · Vedic metres](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/Textual-Studies/Chandas-Sastra/Chandas-Sastra-03.htm)

## Module 2: Light and heavy: every common case

Learn the causes of weight, with contrasts and counterexamples.

### 5. The light case: know the baseline

**Outcome:** Explain when a short vowel remains laghu.

A short vowel is the starting point for a light syllable. It stays light when no following closure or cluster makes it heavy. Within a continuous phrase, a single consonant before the next vowel does not normally make the preceding short vowel heavy.

Use L for laghu and G for guru. We will always give the reason as well as the label.

**Cases and examples**

- **कवि → क · वि | ka · vi** — Both vowels are short. Ka is L before the single v. Vi is naturally L when considered apart from any following context or metrical ending.
- **गुरु → गु · रु | gu · ru** — Both syllables are naturally light in isolation. The word guru, meaning heavy or teacher, does not itself consist of two heavy syllables.
- **कृपा → कृ · पा | kṛ · pā** — Kṛ is L before one consonant p; pā is G because ā is long.

**Go deeper**

Weight is contextual. A dictionary word does not always have one unchanging pattern in connected verse. For now identify natural weight away from a pāda boundary; later we will distinguish it from the metrical treatment of the final position. This prevents the misleading rule that every final written short vowel must always count L.

**Reflect:** Why is kṛ in kṛpā light?

**Explanation:** Ṛ is short, and only one consonant, p, comes before the next vowel.

Sources: [Vṛttaratnākara · Definitions](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/Textual-Studies/Vrttaratnakara/1-Samjnabhidhana.htm); [Apte · Sanskrit Prosody (1890)](https://www.sanskrit-lexicon.uni-koeln.de/scans/csldev/csldoc/build/dictionaries/prefaces/ap90app1.html)

### 6. Heavy because the vowel is long

**Outcome:** Distinguish vowel length from added consonants.

A long vowel makes its syllable heavy. You do not need another reason. A long vowel followed by a cluster is still one heavy syllable; it does not become a four-unit syllable in ordinary Classical scansion.

**Cases and examples**

- **सीता → सी · ता | sī · tā** — G G: ī and ā are both long.
- **देव → दे · व | de · va** — G L in natural isolation: e is long; final a is short.
- **कौशल → कौ · श · ल | kau · śa · la** — G L L in natural isolation. Au is one long vowel, not a + u counted separately.
- **कान्ता → का · न्ता | kā · ntā** — G G. The first syllable has both a long vowel and following nt; it is not counted twice.

**Go deeper**

A useful explanation stores causes separately from the result: kā is heavy by vowel length, and a following cluster also confirms weight. The result remains G = two mātrās for ordinary metrical bookkeeping. Keeping causes visible makes later spelling variants and sandhi changes easier to understand.

**Reflect:** How many mātrās does kā in kāntā contribute to ordinary scansion?

**Explanation:** Two, not four. Multiple causes of heaviness do not add separate penalties.

Sources: [Vṛttaratnākara · Definitions](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/Textual-Studies/Vrttaratnakara/1-Samjnabhidhana.htm); [Apte · Sanskrit Prosody (1890)](https://www.sanskrit-lexicon.uni-koeln.de/scans/csldev/csldoc/build/dictionaries/prefaces/ap90app1.html)

### 7. Heavy before a consonant cluster

**Outcome:** Look forward from the vowel, not at the size of the letter.

A short vowel followed by two or more consonants before the next vowel is normally heavy. The cluster affects the preceding vowel’s syllable. Its printed position can mislead you: a cluster at the start of a syllable does not by itself make that syllable heavy.

**Cases and examples**

- **अग्नि → अ · ग्नि | a · gni** — A is G before g + n. Gni is naturally L: its i is short and the initial gn does not make that i heavy.
- **मित्र → मि · त्र | mi · tra** — Mi is G before t + r. Tra is naturally L in isolation.
- **बुद्ध → बु · द्ध | bu · ddha** — Bu is G before d + dh. Dh is one aspirated consonant; ddh represents two consonants.
- **कथा → क · था | ka · thā** — Ka is L: th is one aspirated consonant, not a cluster t + h. Thā is G from ā.

**Go deeper**

The relevant unit is a consonant sound. Kh, gh, ch, jh, ṭh, ḍh, th, dh, ph and bh each represent one aspirated consonant in IAST. Kṣ, tr and jñ are clusters. Double consonants matter too. The small set of attested poetic exceptions will be taught separately, so the normal rule stays dependable.

**Reflect:** Which first syllable is heavy: kathā or kattā?

**Explanation:** Kattā: tt is a cluster. In kathā, th is a single aspirated consonant and ka remains light. These are sound contrasts, not a vocabulary exercise.

Sources: [Vṛttaratnākara · Definitions](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/Textual-Studies/Vrttaratnakara/1-Samjnabhidhana.htm); [Apte · Sanskrit Prosody (1890)](https://www.sanskrit-lexicon.uni-koeln.de/scans/csldev/csldoc/build/dictionaries/prefaces/ap90app1.html)

### 8. Anusvāra, visarga and final closure

**Outcome:** Handle the other common causes of heaviness.

A syllable with anusvāra ṃ or visarga ḥ is heavy in ordinary scansion. Neither sign adds a new syllable. A genuinely closed syllable at a pause is heavy too; when words connect, inspect the resulting sound sequence rather than automatically closing every written word.

**Cases and examples**

- **संस्कृतम् → सं · स्कृ · तम् | saṃ · skṛ · tam** — G L G at a pause. Saṃ has anusvāra; skṛ has short ṛ followed by one t; tam is closed by final m.
- **दुःख → दुः · ख | duḥ · kha** — G L in natural isolation. Visarga makes duḥ heavy; it does not add a vowel after u.
- **रामः → रा · मः | rā · maḥ** — G G: the first from long ā, the second from visarga.
- **तत् | tat, at a pause** — One closed, heavy syllable. Do not pronounce a new a after t.

**Go deeper**

Anusvāra may be represented or pronounced through a homorganic nasal before a stop; the nasal-plus-stop sequence still supports heaviness. Candrabindu marks nasalization and should not simply be processed as another spelling of anusvāra in every tradition. For unusual notation, consult the source’s pronunciation conventions.

**Reflect:** What is wrong with calling saṃskṛtam G L L at a pause?

**Explanation:** The last syllable tam is closed by m and is heavy. The opening sk cluster does not make skṛ heavy; the final closure does make tam heavy.

Sources: [Vṛttaratnākara · Definitions](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/Textual-Studies/Vrttaratnakara/1-Samjnabhidhana.htm); [LearnSanskrit.org · Syllables](https://www.learnsanskrit.org/guide/core/syllables/)

## Module 3: What context changes

Read sandhi, boundaries, endings and genuine exceptions.

### 9. Word boundaries do not stop the rule

**Outcome:** Scan the connected phrase.

Spaces help us read words, but the sounds often continue across them. A final consonant of one word and an initial consonant of the next can form the cluster that makes a preceding short vowel heavy. Conversely, a final consonant before a vowel can join that following vowel in continuous pronunciation.

**Cases and examples**

- **तत् फलम् | tat phalam** — The a in tat is followed by t + ph before the next vowel. It is heavy. Ph counts as one consonant, giving two in total.
- **तदपि | tad api → ta · da · pi** — The first a is followed by one d before the next vowel. Ta is light in this continuous phrase.
- **कुरुक्षेत्रे | ku · ru · kṣe · tre** — Ku is L before r alone. Ru is G before k + ṣ. Kṣe and tre are G from long e.

**Go deeper**

First decide what text is actually being read: the continuous form with sandhi, a word-separated aid, or a deliberately paused recitation. A grammatical word division is not automatically a metrical pause. Scan the continuous verse, and show the separated form alongside it only as a meaning aid.

**Reflect:** Can the same short vowel change weight when the next word changes?

**Explanation:** Yes. Its following consonant environment can change. This is why whole-phrase examples must follow isolated-word examples.

Sources: [Vṛttaratnākara · Definitions](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/Textual-Studies/Vrttaratnakara/1-Samjnabhidhana.htm); [Apte · Sanskrit Prosody (1890)](https://www.sanskrit-lexicon.uni-koeln.de/scans/csldev/csldoc/build/dictionaries/prefaces/ap90app1.html)

### 10. Sandhi changes what you count

**Outcome:** Keep the reading text and grammatical analysis side by side.

Sandhi joins sounds at word boundaries. It may merge two vowels into one or change the consonants you must inspect. Count the vowels in the form the verse actually uses, not in an expanded word-by-word gloss.

**Cases and examples**

- **देव + आलय → देवालय | deva + ālaya → devālaya** — The boundary a + ā becomes ā. The two separate words have five vowels in total; the combined form has four syllables: de-vā-la-ya.
- **च + एव → चैव | ca + eva → caiva** — Three vowels across the separated forms become two syllables in caiva: cai-va. Ai is one long vowel.
- **ते + अपि → तेऽपि | te + api → te’pi** — The avagraha marks the elided a. It is not an extra syllable: the written connected form has te-pi.

**Go deeper**

Do not apply every conceivable sandhi merely to force a match. Start with an edited text. Where an edition preserves hiatus, separates forms for instruction, or reports alternatives, record that fact. For Vedic texts the relation between the received wording, sandhi and metrical reconstruction needs its own treatment.

**Reflect:** Should the avagraha in te’pi receive its own L or G?

**Explanation:** No. It marks an omitted vowel, not a pronounced extra syllable.

Sources: [LearnSanskrit.org · Syllables](https://www.learnsanskrit.org/guide/core/syllables/); [Apte · Sanskrit Prosody (1890)](https://www.sanskrit-lexicon.uni-koeln.de/scans/csldev/csldoc/build/dictionaries/prefaces/ap90app1.html)

### 11. The last position is special

**Outcome:** Separate natural weight from metrical allowance.

At a pāda ending, a naturally light syllable can often occupy a position treated as heavy. Mark it as final allowance rather than pretending its vowel became long everywhere. X is useful for an ending that permits either weight.

This is an ending rule, not permission to replace light and heavy freely inside the line.

**Cases and examples**

- **सञ्जय | sañ · ja · ya, at the end of Gītā 1.1** — The final ya is naturally light. The śloka ending permits it; the verse need not be “corrected” to yā.
- **Interior कु in कुरुक्षेत्रे** — Ku is not at the pāda end. You cannot use final allowance to make it heavy.
- **Pattern ending … L G L X** — X records an indifferent final position in our teaching notation. It is not a third kind of Sanskrit vowel.

**Go deeper**

Traditional summaries often state the final option broadly. Actual usage differs with the metre and with odd versus even pāda endings; some forms explicitly prescribe a light ending. Keep the observed pronunciation and the pattern match as separate annotations. When a rare case is doubtful, check that metre’s examples and commentary instead of invoking a universal exception.

**Reflect:** May you lengthen any short syllable until a verse fits?

**Explanation:** No. Identify a specific permitted position or attested license. Otherwise retain the mismatch and investigate it.

Sources: [Vṛttaratnākara · Definitions](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/Textual-Studies/Vrttaratnakara/1-Samjnabhidhana.htm); [Rajagopalan · Metrical analysis](https://gallium.inria.fr/~huet/PUBLIC/WSC2018.pdf#page=142)

### 12. Exceptions without destroying the rule

**Outcome:** Recognize a documented license and resist inventing one.

A small number of passages allow a short vowel before certain consonant combinations to scan light. Learn these after the normal cluster rule. An exception is tied to evidence in a text and its metrical tradition; it is not an alternative pronunciation menu.

**Cases and examples**

- **sundari grāmyajanaḥ in Vṛttaratnākara 1.11** — The primary example scans final ri of sundari light before gr. This demonstrates a licensed exception to ordinary cluster weight.
- **अग्नि | agni** — Continue to treat a as heavy before gn. The existence of some cluster exceptions does not weaken every cluster.
- **A line with one extra vowel** — Cluster license changes weight, not the number of vowel nuclei. It will not repair a wrong syllable count.

**Go deeper**

When a scan fails, investigate in order: your vowel count, aspirates versus clusters, sandhi, quarter boundaries, the edition, then a documented license. A manuscript variant and a poetic license are different explanations. Preserve the source reading and the reason for any alternative analysis; never silently replace the text to make the pattern look clean.

**Reflect:** What evidence should support an unusual light syllable before a cluster?

**Explanation:** A relevant metrical rule or attested usage with a reliable edition or commentary—not merely the desire to obtain a familiar pattern.

Sources: [Vṛttaratnākara · Definitions](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/Textual-Studies/Vrttaratnakara/1-Samjnabhidhana.htm); [Apte · Sanskrit Prosody (1890)](https://www.sanskrit-lexicon.uni-koeln.de/scans/csldev/csldoc/build/dictionaries/prefaces/ap90app1.html)

## Module 4: Scan an entire verse

Move from individual reasons to patterns and metre families.

### 13. One pāda, every reason visible

**Outcome:** Complete a scan without guessing from the metre.

Begin with धर्मक्षेत्रे कुरुक्षेत्रे, the opening pāda of Bhagavadgītā 1.1. Count eight vowels. Now assign weight from left to right, giving a reason at each position. Only after that should you compare the result with a metrical pattern.

**Cases and examples**

- **ध · र्म · क्षे · त्रे | dha · rma · kṣe · tre** — G G G G. Dha is heavy before r+m; rma before k+ṣ; kṣe and tre contain long e.
- **कु · रु · क्षे · त्रे | ku · ru · kṣe · tre** — L G G G. Ku is light before r alone; ru is heavy before k+ṣ; both e vowels are long.
- **G G G G L G G G** — Eight syllables, seven heavy and one light: 7×2 + 1 = 15 mātrās. Eight syllables does not mean eight equal time units.

**Go deeper**

The traditional spelling division dha-rma is not claiming that dha is light. Weight looks ahead to the consonants between its vowel and the next vowel. An alternative phonetic division dhar-ma gives the same result. Always be consistent about which display convention you use and explain how it relates to sound.

**Reflect:** Why is the sixth syllable ru heavy while the fifth ku is light?

**Explanation:** Ru is followed by the consonants k and ṣ; ku is followed only by r. The vowel u itself is short in both.

Sources: [LearnSanskrit.org · Syllables](https://www.learnsanskrit.org/guide/core/syllables/); [Apte · Sanskrit Prosody (1890)](https://www.sanskrit-lexicon.uni-koeln.de/scans/csldev/csldoc/build/dictionaries/prefaces/ap90app1.html)

### 14. Two counts tell different stories

**Outcome:** Compare akṣara count with mātrā count.

Syllable count asks how many vowel nuclei occur. Mātrā count assigns one to L and two to G in ordinary metrical analysis. Two sequences may have the same number of syllables but different duration totals—or the same duration total but different numbers of syllables.

**Cases and examples**

- **L L L L and G G G G** — Both contain four syllables. Their totals are four and eight mātrās.
- **G L L and G G** — Both total four mātrās, but the first has three syllables and the second two. They are not freely interchangeable in a fixed syllabic metre.
- **G G G G L G G G** — The Gītā opening has eight syllables and fifteen mātrās. Śloka is recognized through syllabic structure, not a universal fifteen-mātrā quota.

**Go deeper**

Before identifying a metre, decide which system is being tested. In a varṇavṛtta, syllable positions and their weight matter. In a mātrā metre, totals and internal arrangements matter. “Same total” alone proves neither kind of match. Final allowances need explicit treatment rather than silently changing the arithmetic.

**Reflect:** Can G G replace G L L anywhere because both total four?

**Explanation:** No. It changes the syllable count and pattern. Only a metre whose rules permit the relevant alternatives can accept that substitution.

Sources: [Vṛttaratnākara · Definitions](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/Textual-Studies/Vrttaratnakara/1-Samjnabhidhana.htm); [Vṛttaratnākara · Āryā](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/Textual-Studies/Vrttaratnakara/2-Matravrtta-01.htm)

### 15. Gaṇas: useful shorthand, not a new obstacle

**Outcome:** Read the eight three-syllable groups.

A gaṇa compresses three weight symbols into a name. Learn the patterns by comparison, then use the names to read traditional definitions. You can scan correctly without memorizing the names first.

**Cases and examples**

- **ma = GGG · na = LLL** — The two uniform groups: all heavy, all light.
- **ya = LGG · ra = GLG · ta = GGL** — One light syllable: at the beginning, middle or end.
- **bha = GLL · ja = LGL · sa = LLG** — One heavy syllable: at the beginning, middle or end.
- **GGG | GLG | GG → ma · ra · G · G** — Our eight-syllable opening divides into two groups of three and two leftover heavies. Do not discard the leftovers.

**Go deeper**

A gaṇa division is analytical, not a command to pause every three syllables. A three-syllable group may cross a word or a prescribed yati. Mātrā metres also use the word gaṇa for groups of time units; those are not automatically these eight three-syllable patterns. Always identify which grouping system is in use.

**Reflect:** What is LGL, and must you pause after it?

**Explanation:** Ja-gaṇa. No: its analytical boundary need not coincide with a word boundary or a recitation pause.

Sources: [Vṛttaratnākara · Definitions](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/Textual-Studies/Vrttaratnakara/1-Samjnabhidhana.htm); [Apte · Sanskrit Prosody (1890)](https://www.sanskrit-lexicon.uni-koeln.de/scans/csldev/csldoc/build/dictionaries/prefaces/ap90app1.html)

### 16. A map of metre families

**Outcome:** Choose the right kind of comparison.

First ask whether a verse is regulated mainly by syllable positions or by mātrā groups. Next ask how its pādas relate. This family map is more useful than memorizing a long list of names with no structure.

**Cases and examples**

- **Samavṛtta: A / A / A / A** — All four pādas follow the same prescription. Bhujaṅgaprayāta is a clear example.
- **Ardhasamavṛtta: A / B / A / B** — The first and third follow one prescription; the second and fourth follow another. Puṣpitāgrā is an example.
- **Viṣamavṛtta: prescribed unequal quarters** — The quarters differ according to the metre’s definition. “Unequal” does not mean anything goes.
- **Mātrā metre: Āryā** — The familiar quarter totals are 12 / 18 / 12 / 15, with additional internal rules. It is a different kind of measurement.

**Go deeper**

Śloka needs its own treatment because its permitted openings and alternating cadences are not a single repeated fixed L/G string. Traditional books also differ in how they organize material: do not infer that a metre is mātrā-based simply because it appears in a chapter alongside mātrā metres. Classification is a tool for testing the actual rules.

**Reflect:** Four pādas have identical syllable counts. Does that prove samavṛtta?

**Explanation:** No. Their weight prescriptions may differ, or they may belong to a family with variable openings. Compare the full rules.

## Module 5: Understand the śloka

Learn the normal cadence, its restrictions and named variations.

### 17. The familiar śloka cadence

**Outcome:** Recognize the pathyā pattern without overgeneralizing.

The common Classical śloka has four eight-syllable pādas. In its normal, pathyā form, the closing rhythm differs between odd pādas (1 and 3) and even pādas (2 and 4). Learn to hear that alternation before adding the opening restrictions.

**Cases and examples**

- **Odd pādas: … | L G G X** — Positions 5–7 are light, heavy, heavy in the ordinary pathyā cadence. Position 8 is the ending.
- **Even pādas: … | L G L X** — The seventh position is light in the normal even cadence. That alternating seventh syllable helps distinguish the two halves.
- **धर्मक्षेत्रे कुरुक्षेत्रे → GGGG | LGGG** — The Gītā opening is a normal odd cadence. Its sixth syllable is heavy because of the following cluster, not a long vowel.

**Go deeper**

The dots do not mean that absolutely any opening is legal. Pathyā has opening restrictions, and recognized vipulās alter the ordinary odd-pāda shape. Thus “every śloka has a light fifth and a heavy sixth” is a useful first mnemonic but an incomplete general rule. Next we make the restrictions explicit and then study the variations.

**Reflect:** What distinguishes the ordinary odd and even cadences?

**Explanation:** Their seventh position: G in the odd cadence, L in the even cadence. The fifth is L and sixth G in this normal form.

Sources: [Vṛttaratnākara · Vaktra](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/Textual-Studies/Vrttaratnakara/2-Matravrtta-04.htm); [Apte · Sanskrit Prosody (1890)](https://www.sanskrit-lexicon.uni-koeln.de/scans/csldev/csldoc/build/dictionaries/prefaces/ap90app1.html)

### 18. Eight syllables are not enough

**Outcome:** Test the opening as well as the ending.

A stanza is not a śloka merely because its four quarters each have eight syllables. In the standard Classical pattern, positions 2 and 3 must not both be light. The usual even-pāda opening also avoids a ja-gaṇa, LGL, in positions 2–4. These restrictions explain why some plausible-looking eight-syllable strings fail.

**Cases and examples**

- **G G G G | L G G X** — A compatible ordinary odd shape. It has the expected cadence and does not put two light syllables in positions 2–3.
- **G L L G | L G G X** — A constructed near-miss. The closing cadence looks right, but positions 2–3 are both L.
- **G L G L | L G L X** — Another constructed near-miss for an ordinary even pāda: positions 2–4 are LGL. These are patterns for comparison, not quotations.

**Go deeper**

Apply these as the course’s standard Classical śloka baseline. Epic and older usage contains additional freedom, and named variants have their own opening and word-break conditions. Keep a classification such as “standard pathyā,” “identified variant,” or “needs investigation”; a binary pass/fail based on syllable count hides the important distinctions.

**Reflect:** Why does an eight-syllable line with the correct last four still need checking?

**Explanation:** Its opening may violate a restriction. A successful cadence is only one part of the test.

Sources: [Vṛttaratnākara · Definitions](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/Textual-Studies/Vrttaratnakara/1-Samjnabhidhana.htm); [Vṛttaratnākara · Vaktra](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/Textual-Studies/Vrttaratnakara/2-Matravrtta-04.htm)

### 19. Vipulās: learn variation as a system

**Outcome:** Understand why authentic ślokas need more than one template.

Vipulās are recognized variations, especially in odd pādas. Their characteristic weight groups and word breaks make them structured alternatives. They are not mistakes, and they do not give permission to change an even pāda into any pattern you like.

**Cases and examples**

- **Vṛttaratnākara’s bha-vipulā rule verse: G G L G | G L L G** — Notice the GLL group after position 4, unlike pathyā LGG. The full line is a scanned definition, not a license to combine this ending with any opening.
- **Its ra-vipulā rule verse: G L G G | G L G G** — The GLG group follows position 4. Word division is relevant when recognizing the variant.
- **Its na-vipulā rule verse: G L G G | L L L G** — The LLL group changes the cadence markedly. Count and inspect the whole pāda.
- **Its ta-vipulā rule verse: G G G G | G G L G** — Here the book names the GGL group at positions 5–7. Other accounts discuss a ma-vipulā using a GGG group spanning positions 4–6. Grouping conventions matter.

**Go deeper**

Read the labels together with their author’s definition. Common treatments discuss na-, bha-, ma- and ra-vipulā; Kedārabhaṭṭa’s Vaktra section includes additional names and a ta-based description. Do not merge all labels into interchangeable endings. For an unfamiliar line, record the complete opening, cadence and word breaks, then compare a source’s full account and examples.

**Reflect:** If a real śloka has a heavy fifth syllable, is it automatically corrupt?

**Explanation:** No. A recognized vipulā may explain it. Check the whole odd pāda and the applicable definition before proposing an error.

Sources: [Vṛttaratnākara · Vaktra](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/Textual-Studies/Vrttaratnakara/2-Matravrtta-04.htm)

### 20. Read a complete śloka

**Outcome:** Bring sound, pattern and meaning together.

Return to Bhagavadgītā 1.1. Dhṛtarāṣṭra asks Sañjaya what his people and the Pāṇḍavas did after assembling at Kurukṣetra, wishing to fight. The metrical divisions organize the question without replacing its grammar.

**Cases and examples**

- **धर्मक्षेत्रे कुरुक्षेत्रे · GGGG | LGGG** — dha-rma-kṣe-tre ku-ru-kṣe-tre. Eight syllables; normal odd cadence.
- **समवेता युयुत्सवः · LLGG | LGLG** — sa-ma-ve-tā yu-yu-tsa-vaḥ. The second yu is heavy before t+s. Normal even cadence.
- **मामकाः पाण्डवाश्चैव · GLGG | LGGL** — mā-ma-kāḥ pā-ṇḍa-vā-ścai-va. The final va is naturally light and allowed at the ending.
- **किमकुर्वत सञ्जय · LLGL | LGLX** — ki-ma-ku-rva-ta sa-ñja-ya. Ku is heavy before r+v; sa is heavy before ñ+j; final ya uses ending allowance.

**Go deeper**

Now separate reading from analysis. Read each pāda fluently, without inserting pauses at every gaṇa. Explain one weight caused by vowel length, one by a cluster and one by visarga. Then paraphrase the question. A learner who can give both the meaning and the reasons has learned more than someone who merely recognizes “Anuṣṭubh.”

**Reflect:** Which two final syllables in this verse show that ending allowance matters?

**Explanation:** Va in caiva, at the third pāda ending, and ya in Sañjaya, at the fourth. Both are naturally light.

## Module 6: Meet the classical families

Compare short and medium metres through their defining differences.

### 21. Start with a repeating twelve-syllable pattern

**Outcome:** Distinguish Bhujaṅgaprayāta, Toṭaka and Sragviṇī.

A repeating group makes a good first fixed metre. Bhujaṅgaprayāta repeats LGG four times per pāda. Compare it with two equally regular twelve-syllable patterns; the differences become easy to locate when they are aligned.

**Cases and examples**

- **Bhujaṅgaprayāta: LGG / LGG / LGG / LGG** — Four ya-gaṇas. Each group has five mātrās, but the defining pattern is twelve syllable positions.
- **Toṭaka: LLG / LLG / LLG / LLG** — Four sa-gaṇas. The heavy syllable moves to the end of each group.
- **Sragviṇī: GLG / GLG / GLG / GLG** — Four ra-gaṇas. Each group again has five mātrās, but the pattern differs from Bhujaṅgaprayāta.

**Go deeper**

Compare Bhujaṅgaprayāta with Sragviṇī: both have twelve syllables and twenty mātrās per pāda, yet they are distinct metres. This is a strong counterexample to identification by totals alone. Speak the abstract groups first; then read the Sanskrit definition bhujaṅgaprayātaṃ bhaved yaiś caturbhiḥ, which embodies its own pattern.

**Reflect:** Can twenty mātrās and twelve syllables identify Bhujaṅgaprayāta uniquely?

**Explanation:** No. Sragviṇī has the same totals. The ordering of L and G distinguishes them.

Sources: [Vṛttaratnākara · 12 syllables](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/Textual-Studies/Vrttaratnakara/3-Samavrtta-12.htm)

### 22. One position changes the family resemblance

**Outcome:** Compare Indravajrā, Upendravajrā and Upajāti.

Indravajrā and Upendravajrā have eleven syllables per pāda. They differ at the first position. A stanza that mixes their pādas is the familiar form of Upajāti. This teaches comparison through one controlled difference rather than memorizing three disconnected names.

**Cases and examples**

- **Indravajrā: GGL / GGL / LGL / G G** — Ta, ta, ja, G, G. The first syllable is heavy.
- **Upendravajrā: LGL / GGL / LGL / G G** — Ja, ta, ja, G, G. Only the first syllable changes to light.
- **अस्त्युत्तरस्यां दिशि देवतात्मा** — Kumārasambhava 1.1 opens with an Indravajrā pāda: as-tyut-ta-ra-syāṃ di-śi de-va-tā-tmā.
- **हिमालयो नाम नगाधिराजः** — The next pāda is Upendravajrā: hi-mā-la-yo nā-ma na-gā-dhi-rā-jaḥ. The stanza belongs to the familiar Upajāti family.

**Go deeper**

The two lines introduce Himālaya as the divine-souled king of mountains in the north. Upajāti is also used more broadly by some prosodists for mixtures of other related metres. Name the specific pair when precision matters. Do not infer a stanza’s classification from its first pāda alone.

**Reflect:** What must you inspect before calling a stanza Upajāti?

**Explanation:** All its pādas and the particular forms they use. A single eleven-syllable line cannot establish a mixed stanza.

Sources: [Vṛttaratnākara · 11 syllables](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/Textual-Studies/Vrttaratnakara/3-Samavrtta-11.htm); [Apte · Sanskrit Prosody (1890)](https://www.sanskrit-lexicon.uni-koeln.de/scans/csldev/csldoc/build/dictionaries/prefaces/ap90app1.html)

### 23. Other medium metres: compare before naming

**Outcome:** Use a few characteristic patterns to navigate the catalogue.

Not all metres consist of one repeated gaṇa. Some share a long opening sequence and diverge only near the end. Group them by length first, then compare the positions that actually distinguish them.

**Cases and examples**

- **Vaṃśastha: LGL / GGL / LGL / GLG** — Twelve syllables: ja, ta, ja, ra. Compare its last three with the eleven-syllable Upendravajrā ending.
- **Drutavilambita: LLL / GLL / GLL / GLG** — Twelve syllables: na, bha, bha, ra. Its run of initial lights creates a clearly different opening.
- **Rathoddhatā: GLG / LLL / GLG / L G** — Eleven syllables: ra, na, ra, L, G. Count the two leftover positions.
- **Śālinī: GGG / GGL / GGL / G G** — Eleven syllables: ma, ta, ta, G, G; its usual internal division follows the fourth syllable.

**Go deeper**

A metre’s name can suggest an image or movement, but the name is not a measurable tempo instruction. Use the exact pattern and any yati requirement. Comparing neighbours helps retrieval: “eleven syllables, heavy opening, fourth-position division” narrows the search much more reliably than remembering a vague mood.

**Reflect:** What separates Vaṃśastha from a generic twelve-syllable verse?

**Explanation:** Its specific L/G arrangement. Twelve is a useful first filter, not the definition.

Sources: [Vṛttaratnākara · 11 syllables](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/Textual-Studies/Vrttaratnakara/3-Samavrtta-11.htm); [Vṛttaratnākara · 12 syllables](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/Textual-Studies/Vrttaratnakara/3-Samavrtta-12.htm)

### 24. Alternating and unequal quarters

**Outcome:** Recognize structure beyond four identical lines.

In an alternating metre, the first and third pādas share one pattern while the second and fourth share another. You must compare the right partners. A line that differs from the first is not necessarily an error.

**Cases and examples**

- **Puṣpitāgrā · odd: LLL / LLL / GLG / LGG** — Twelve syllables: na, na, ra, ya.
- **Puṣpitāgrā · even: LLL / LGL / LGL / GLG / G** — Thirteen syllables: na, ja, ja, ra, G. The stanza alternates twelve and thirteen.
- **Viṣamavṛtta · a prescribed sequence** — Vṛttaratnākara gives forms with unequal quarters, including a progressive 8, 12, 16, 20-syllable scheme. Different lengths can be part of the rule.

**Go deeper**

Write four separate rows before matching. First compare 1 with 3 and 2 with 4; then consult the named definition. Distinguish alternating fixed metres from a mixture such as the familiar Upajāti and from mātrā-based Āryā. Similar-looking unequal lengths do not make these the same category.

**Reflect:** In Puṣpitāgrā, is a thirteen-syllable second pāda a counting mistake?

**Explanation:** Not if it has the prescribed even-pāda pattern. Its thirteen syllables are part of the alternating design.

## Module 7: Read longer metres

Follow longer patterns without losing phrasing or meaning.

### 25. Vasantatilakā and Mālinī

**Outcome:** Read longer patterns in meaningful sections.

With longer pādas, keep two maps: the gaṇa shorthand for checking and the phrase divisions for reading. Vasantatilakā has fourteen syllables; Mālinī has fifteen. Their differing openings are easy to notice even before learning the names.

**Cases and examples**

- **Vasantatilakā: GGL / GLL / LGL / LGL / G G** — Ta, bha, ja, ja, G, G. Four groups of three plus two equals fourteen. Apte gives an 8 + 6 division.
- **Mālinī: LLL / LLL / GGG / LGG / LGG** — Na, na, ma, ya, ya. Five groups of three equals fifteen; the conventional division is 8 + 7.
- **Same analytical tool, different phrase boundary** — The eight-syllable division cuts across a three-syllable gaṇa boundary. That is normal: gaṇas and yati serve different purposes.

**Go deeper**

To learn either pattern, first mark the vowels on a source line, then label each weight independently. Compare the resulting string with the formula. Finally put the text back into phrases and read for meaning. Working backwards from a known metre can help diagnose uncertainty, but should not replace the independent scan.

**Reflect:** Does an 8 + 6 division tell you all the weights of Vasantatilakā?

**Explanation:** No. It tells you the section lengths. You still need the GGL GLL LGL LGL GG pattern.

Sources: [Vṛttaratnākara · 14 syllables](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/Textual-Studies/Vrttaratnakara/3-Samavrtta-14.htm); [Vṛttaratnākara · 15 syllables](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/Textual-Studies/Vrttaratnakara/3-Samavrtta-15.htm); [Apte · Sanskrit Prosody (1890)](https://www.sanskrit-lexicon.uni-koeln.de/scans/csldev/csldoc/build/dictionaries/prefaces/ap90app1.html)

### 26. Mandākrāntā: a long line you can navigate

**Outcome:** Use the 4 + 6 + 7 structure to read seventeen syllables.

Mandākrāntā has seventeen syllables per pāda. Its four-heavy opening and following run of lights give a memorable contrast. Work with its three reading sections before trying to memorize the full string.

**Cases and examples**

- **GGGG | LLLLLG | GLGGLGG** — Four syllables, then six, then seven. Total: seventeen. This is the same pattern as ma, bha, na, ta, ta, G, G.
- **कश्चित् कान्ताविरहगुरुणा स्वाधिकारप्रमत्तः** — The opening of Meghadūta 1, in the svādhikārapramattaḥ reading attested by the linked manuscript. A yakṣa has neglected his duty and is burdened by separation from his beloved.
- **kaś-cit-kān-tā | vi-ra-ha-gu-ru-ṇā | svā-dhi-kā-ra-pra-mat-taḥ** — The middle section gives five light syllables and a long ṇā. Ra in the final section is heavy before pr. Mat is heavy before tt; taḥ has visarga.

**Go deeper**

The first internal division comes inside the compound kāntā-viraha. A metrical division may align with a compound-member boundary rather than a space between printed words. Do not automatically insert a dramatic breath at every analytical divider. Different editions have svādhikārāt pramattaḥ; record which reading you use instead of silently mixing them.

**Reflect:** Why is ra in svādhikārapramattaḥ heavy?

**Explanation:** Its a is short, but p+r follows before the next vowel. This is ordinary cluster weight in the reading used here.

Sources: [Vṛttaratnākara · 17 syllables](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/Textual-Studies/Vrttaratnakara/3-Samavrtta-17.htm); [Hamburg manuscript catalogue · Meghadūta 1](https://www-archiv.fdm.uni-hamburg.de/ngmcp/A_386-17_Meghad%25C5%25ABta.html)

### 27. Śikhariṇī, Pṛthvī and Hariṇī

**Outcome:** Distinguish three metres of the same length.

Seventeen syllables do not identify Mandākrāntā. Several important metres share that length. Compare their opening, internal divisions and ending. The point is to learn how to consult a pattern, not to memorize every name at once.

**Cases and examples**

- **Śikhariṇī: LGG / GGG / LLL / LLG / GLL / L G** — Ya, ma, na, sa, bha, L, G. Its conventional division is 6 + 11.
- **Pṛthvī: LGL / LLG / LGL / LLG / LGG / L G** — Ja, sa, ja, sa, ya, L, G. Division: 8 + 9.
- **Hariṇī: LLL / LLG / GGG / GLG / LLG / L G** — Na, sa, ma, ra, sa, L, G. Divisions: 6 + 4 + 7.

**Go deeper**

Put the patterns on aligned syllable positions when comparing them. A different yati can also distinguish metres with closely related or identical weight strings in traditional catalogues. Identification therefore includes both a sound pattern and the structural information supplied by its definition. A name-only answer loses that evidence.

**Reflect:** A scan has seventeen syllables and begins LGGGGG. What is a useful next candidate?

**Explanation:** Śikhariṇī: its ya + ma opening is LGGGGG. Check the remaining eleven positions and the division; the opening alone is not proof.

Sources: [Vṛttaratnākara · 17 syllables](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/Textual-Studies/Vrttaratnakara/3-Samavrtta-17.htm)

### 28. Longer lines and the edge of the catalogue

**Outcome:** Read nineteen and twenty-one syllables; understand daṇḍaka.

Long metres become manageable when you preserve their internal organization. Learn two widely encountered patterns, then learn how to use the catalogue for rarer lengths. You do not need a separate memorization lesson for every theoretical L/G combination.

**Cases and examples**

- **Śārdūlavikrīḍita: GGG / LLG / LGL / LLG / GGL / GGL / G** — Ma, sa, ja, sa, ta, ta, G: nineteen syllables, conventionally divided 12 + 7.
- **Sragdharā: GGG / GLG / GLL / LLL / LGG / LGG / LGG** — Ma, ra, bha, na, ya, ya, ya: twenty-one syllables, divided 7 + 7 + 7.
- **Daṇḍaka** — Traditional catalogues extend beyond twenty-six syllables per pāda into very long forms called daṇḍaka. They still have prescriptions; length does not make them free verse.

**Go deeper**

Traditional length classes provide an address in the catalogue, not a unique metre name. A twenty-one-syllable class contains more possibilities than Sragdharā. For a rare form, read its defining gaṇas, yati and all four quarters, then examine a cited example. This is a transferable reading skill that covers new metres beyond the selected repertoire.

**Reflect:** How should you approach a twenty-seven-syllable pāda?

**Explanation:** Verify the count and text first, then consult the long-form/daṇḍaka definitions. Do not shorten it just to force it into a familiar metre.

## Module 8: Measure time: mātrā metres

Understand Āryā and related families beyond total counts.

### 29. Mātrā groups: same time, different shapes

**Outcome:** Build the five ordinary four-unit groups.

A mātrā group measures time rather than a fixed number of syllables. With L worth one and G worth two, there are five ways to fill four units. This explains why the syllable count may vary while the time framework stays stable.

**Cases and examples**

- **G G = 2 + 2** — Two syllables fill four mātrās.
- **G L L · L G L · L L G** — Each has three syllables and totals four. The heavy syllable occupies a different position.
- **L L L L = 1 + 1 + 1 + 1** — Four syllables also fill four mātrās.
- **A mātrā gaṇa is not a three-syllable gaṇa** — Only three of these five groups have exactly three syllables. The word gaṇa means a group; its definition depends on context.

**Go deeper**

A metre chooses among these groups and may forbid certain arrangements at particular positions. Equal duration does not guarantee substitutability. For example, Āryā restricts the LGL arrangement in its odd-numbered full groups. Start with the inventory, then apply the metre-specific grammar of groups.

**Reflect:** How can two syllables and four syllables occupy the same mātrā group?

**Explanation:** GG totals four, just as LLLL totals four. The number of syllables differs; the measured total agrees.

Sources: [Vṛttaratnākara · Definitions](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/Textual-Studies/Vrttaratnakara/1-Samjnabhidhana.htm); [Vṛttaratnākara · Āryā](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/Textual-Studies/Vrttaratnakara/2-Matravrtta-01.htm)

### 30. Āryā: understand the whole framework

**Outcome:** Use both the quarter totals and the internal groups.

The familiar Āryā totals are 12, 18, 12 and 15 mātrās. These combine into halves of 30 and 27. But a string that happens to total 57 is not automatically Āryā: its groups and divisions must also obey the rules.

**Cases and examples**

- **First half: GG | GG | LLG || GG | GLL | LGL | LLG | G** — An illustrative skeleton: seven four-mātrā groups and a final G = 30. The first three groups give 12; the remainder gives 18.
- **Second half: GG | GLL | GG || LLG | GLL | L | GG | G** — The sixth group is reduced to a single L: 12 + 15 = 27. These are metrical skeletons, not Sanskrit quotations.
- **The odd full groups exclude LGL** — Groups 1, 3, 5 and 7 are not ja-shaped. The first half’s sixth group has special requirements: LGL or four lights, with appropriate word division.

**Go deeper**

In the first half the sixth group may be LGL, or four lights under the stated division conditions. The second half instead has a single light at that point. The detailed yati and word-boundary provisions matter too. Read the primary definition alongside a marked example; a mātrā counter alone cannot validate Āryā.

**Reflect:** Why is a total of 57 mātrās insufficient?

**Explanation:** It could have the wrong quarter totals, forbidden internal groups, the wrong sixth-group treatment or unsuitable word divisions.

Sources: [Vṛttaratnākara · Āryā](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/Textual-Studies/Vrttaratnakara/2-Matravrtta-01.htm); [Apte · Sanskrit Prosody (1890)](https://www.sanskrit-lexicon.uni-koeln.de/scans/csldev/csldoc/build/dictionaries/prefaces/ap90app1.html)

### 31. Āryā variants and the Gīti family

**Outcome:** Compare related structures without conflating their names.

Once you know the two Āryā halves, several relatives become easy to understand. Repeating or reversing the halves changes the stanza. These family relationships are a better memory aid than a flat list of totals.

**Cases and examples**

- **Āryā: 12 / 18 / 12 / 15** — A 30-mātrā half followed by a 27-mātrā half.
- **Gīti: 12 / 18 / 12 / 18** — Two halves of the first type: 30 + 30.
- **Upagīti: 12 / 15 / 12 / 15** — Two halves of the second type: 27 + 27.
- **Udgīti: 12 / 15 / 12 / 18** — The two types occur in the reverse order: 27 + 30.
- **Āryāgīti: 12 / 20 / 12 / 20** — The extended form has 32 + 32 mātrās. Internal prescriptions still matter.

**Go deeper**

Within Āryā, pathyā and vipulā describe different relationships between the word division and the first three groups. Capalā specifies ja-shaped second and fourth groups with intervening heavy structure; mukhacapalā applies that characteristic to the first half, jaghanacapalā to the second. These names belong to the Āryā system here: a śloka vipulā is a different analysis.

**Reflect:** Does “vipulā” always mean a variation of śloka?

**Explanation:** No. Āryā has its own vipulā, defined through its own group and word-division rules. Always specify the metre family.

### 32. Vaitālīya and other measured families

**Outcome:** Notice that a fixed cadence can follow a variable opening.

Mātrā metres are not all built like Āryā. In Vaitālīya, an opening measured by mātrās leads into a fixed closing sequence. Odd and even pādas have different opening totals. This gives you another model for recognizing unfamiliar verse.

**Cases and examples**

- **Vaitālīya · odd: 6 mātrās + G L G L G** — The five-syllable cadence totals eight mātrās; the pāda totals fourteen.
- **Vaitālīya · even: 8 mātrās + G L G L G** — The same cadence follows a longer opening; the pāda totals sixteen.
- **Aupacchandasika: related opening + G L G L G G** — The extended cadence adds a heavy syllable, giving totals of sixteen and eighteen respectively.
- **Mātrāsamaka and other jāti forms** — Other prescriptions use equal mātrā totals with additional positional rules. Learn the particular definition rather than applying Āryā’s groups to them.

**Go deeper**

These totals and cadences identify the family but are not its entire grammar. Vaitālīya places further restrictions on light-syllable arrangements and group connections; its named subtypes add conditions. Compare the continuous Sanskrit definition across the linked Gīti and Vaitālīya pages. At this stage the goal is sound classification and guided reference use, not unsupported validation of every subtype.

**Reflect:** Does fourteen mātrās in an odd pāda prove Vaitālīya?

**Explanation:** No. The closing GLGLG sequence and the internal opening restrictions must also be satisfied.

## Module 9: Read poetry as poetry

Connect metre with words, syntax, pauses and recitation.

### 33. Yati: a division that helps the listener

**Outcome:** Distinguish metre, word boundaries and breathing.

Yati is an internal division prescribed or recognized within a metrical line. It can help a listener follow a long verse. It is not identical to a gaṇa boundary, a printed space, a punctuation mark or a place where you personally run out of breath.

**Cases and examples**

- **Mandākrāntā: 4 + 6 + 7** — The boundaries follow syllables 4 and 10. The gaṇa groups of three do not land on those same positions.
- **Sragdharā: 7 + 7 + 7** — Three equal sections organize a twenty-one-syllable pāda. Counting each section separately makes a missed vowel easier to locate.
- **A word cut after its first consonant** — Do not manufacture a “pause” inside an indivisible sound unit just because a visual ruler reaches its mark. Inspect the actual word and the metre’s yati rule.

**Go deeper**

Traditional treatments distinguish allowable and faulty word divisions, including questions about compound boundaries. A teaching display should preserve both the whole word and the metrical division. In performance, a boundary may be conveyed by phrasing rather than a long silence. Learn the text’s prescribed organization before experimenting with expressive pauses.

**Reflect:** Must a pause occur after every three-syllable gaṇa?

**Explanation:** No. Gaṇas encode weight; yati organizes the line. Their boundaries often differ.

### 34. Meaning survives the metrical arrangement

**Outcome:** Keep original order, word analysis and prose sense connected.

Poets arrange words to satisfy metre and express meaning. To understand a verse, keep its original order visible, then unpack sandhi and compounds, and finally build a prose-order explanation. Do not replace the original verse with its paraphrase when scanning.

**Cases and examples**

- **वागर्थाविव सम्पृक्तौ | vāgarthāv iva saṃpṛktau** — Raghuvaṃśa 1.1 compares the pair being praised to speech and meaning, closely joined. Vāk + artha appears inside vāgarthau, with sandhi before iva.
- **वागर्थप्रतिपत्तये | vāgarthapratipattaye** — The purpose is understanding speech and meaning. The compound is long, but still has a precise vowel count.
- **जगतः पितरौ वन्दे / पार्वतीपरमेश्वरौ** — The speaker salutes Pārvatī and Parameśvara as the parents of the world. The two objects of reverence are grammatically connected across the verse.

**Go deeper**

Use three synchronized layers: original verse for metre, padaccheda for word recognition, and anvaya or prose ordering for syntax. A compound-member boundary can be meaningful without being a separate grammatical word. This workflow connects the Verse & Rhythm course to grammar and reading without duplicating the same lesson in three separate sections.

**Reflect:** Which version should you scan: the original verse or its prose-order paraphrase?

**Explanation:** The original verse. The paraphrase helps meaning but changes the sound sequence and usually the metre.

### 35. Metre is not rhyme, tune or emotion

**Outcome:** Interpret poetic effects without turning associations into rules.

Sanskrit metrical identity depends on the prescribed pattern, not on matching final sounds. Rhyme, repeated consonants, wordplay, musical tune and expressive tempo can contribute to a poem, but they are separate features.

**Cases and examples**

- **Same ending sound, different pattern** — Two lines can rhyme and still have different syllable counts or weights. Rhyme cannot establish that they share a metre.
- **Same metre, different performance** — A reader can vary pitch and pacing while preserving the syllabic structure. A tune should not erase a short/long distinction in the text.
- **Mandākrāntā in Meghadūta** — Its movement participates in a poem of longing. That literary association does not mean every Mandākrāntā verse must express sorrow or use one fixed melody.

**Go deeper**

Read an effect as a relationship between metre, diction, syntax and context. A string of heavy syllables is observable; calling it solemn is an interpretation that needs the surrounding poem. In this course, pattern labels are technical claims, while suggested emotional readings are invitations to compare what the words and performance do.

**Reflect:** Can you identify a Sanskrit metre from rhyme alone?

**Explanation:** No. Scan the syllables and compare the relevant rules. Rhyme is a separate feature.

### 36. A practical recitation loop

**Outcome:** Move from accurate speech to fluent reading.

Use a short passage whose meaning you understand. Read it plainly, inspect difficult sounds, follow the weight pattern slowly, then return to natural phrasing. Record yourself if useful and compare the actual words before judging expressiveness.

The course’s diagrams show metrical structure; they are not recordings of an authentic chant.

**Cases and examples**

- **Pass 1 · Words** — Read धर्मक्षेत्रे कुरुक्षेत्रे without adding vowels inside kṣ or tr. Keep long e distinct.
- **Pass 2 · Structure** — Explain GGGG LGGG, then read the whole pāda again without stopping after every symbol.
- **Pass 3 · Meaning and continuity** — Read the full Gītā question across four pādas, giving enough phrase shape for a listener to follow it.

**Go deeper**

A useful audio lesson should name the reader and tradition, supply normal and slower recordings, and align the spoken text with the annotation. **Explanation:** The inserted vowel. It changes the sound sequence and possibly the syllable count. Expressive performance comes after an accurate text.

## Module 10: Enter Vedic verse carefully

Distinguish Vedic metre, accent and living recitation traditions.

### 37. Vedic metre: a related but distinct system

**Outcome:** Start with stanza shapes, not Classical templates.

Vedic verse shares the importance of syllables and rhythm, but its forms and conventions need their own treatment. Begin with the main stanza shapes. These are standard reference forms, not a claim that every Vedic occurrence is perfectly regular.

**Cases and examples**

- **Gāyatrī · 8 + 8 + 8 = 24** — Usually three eight-syllable pādas. This is not the same as a Classical catalogue’s six-syllable length-class called Gāyatrī.
- **Uṣṇih · 8 + 8 + 12 = 28; Anuṣṭubh · 4×8 = 32** — A common Uṣṇih arrangement and the familiar four-eight framework. Vedic Anuṣṭubh is not simply the fully developed Classical śloka rule set.
- **Bṛhatī · 8 + 8 + 12 + 8 = 36; Paṅkti · 5×8 = 40** — Common reference forms; placement and other variants exist.
- **Triṣṭubh · 4×11 = 44; Jagatī · 4×12 = 48** — Important Vedic families whose rhythmic structure requires more than the total count.

**Go deeper**

Count supplies the first map, not the whole analysis: cadences, internal divisions, historical phonology and textual tradition also matter. Names can recur in later classifications with different meanings. Always state whether you mean a Vedic stanza type, a Classical length class or a particular fixed Classical metre.

**Reflect:** Does Vedic Triṣṭubh mean every pāda must be Indravajrā?

**Explanation:** No. Indravajrā is a specific Classical eleven-syllable pattern. Vedic Triṣṭubh is a broader historical metrical family.

### 38. Read a three-pāda Vedic stanza

**Outcome:** Count Ṛgveda 1.1.1 without inventing a fourth quarter.

The first Ṛgvedic stanza praises Agni in his ritual roles. Read its three pādas as three units. The Sanskrit below omits accent marks only for the first counting pass; the linked source preserves the accented text and its recitation.

**Cases and examples**

- **अग्निमीळे पुरोहितम् | agnimīḷe purohitam** — Eight vowels: a · i · ī · e · u · o · i · a. Agni is invoked as the priest placed at the forefront.
- **यज्ञस्य देवमृत्विजम् | yajñasya devamṛtvijam** — Eight vowels: a · a · a · e · a · ṛ · i · a. The line continues the description of Agni as divine officiant.
- **होतारं रत्नधातमम् | hotāraṃ ratnadhātamam** — Eight vowels: o · ā · a · a · a · ā · a · a. It completes the praise, including his role as hotṛ and best bestower of treasure.

**Go deeper**

The stanza is Gāyatrī: 8 + 8 + 8. A modern layout may print the first two pādas together. The word-separated forms help meaning, but the continuous received text and its accent marks belong to the recitation lesson. Do not add Oṃ to the metrical count unless you are explicitly analyzing an edition or liturgical setting that includes it as a separate element.

**Reflect:** Why is a four-quarter śloka template inappropriate here?

**Explanation:** This stanza has three eight-syllable pādas. Forcing a fourth misrepresents its Vedic form.

### 39. Accent, duration and recitation tradition

**Outcome:** Keep three dimensions distinct.

Vedic accent is a feature of the transmitted text and its recitation. The traditional categories include udātta, anudātta and svarita. They are not synonyms for guru, laghu or a musical beat. A heavy syllable can carry an accent, but weight alone does not determine it.

**Cases and examples**

- **Weight asks: how is the syllable measured?** — Long vowels and following consonants matter for the metrical analysis.
- **Accent asks: what accentual category is transmitted?** — Use the accented text and the conventions of the named Veda and śākhā. A mark’s interpretation cannot be generalized from one tutorial to every tradition.
- **Recitation asks: how is this version performed?** — Compare the Śākala Ṛgveda text of 1.1 with its linked recitation. Observe the same words before attempting to copy pitch.

**Go deeper**

The actual realization of accent and the use of written marks vary among recitation traditions. This preview therefore does not invent a universal three-note tune or infer accent from L/G. A specialist branch should pair a named, reviewed recitation model with its matching marked text. Pluta and tradition-specific phonetic details belong in that branch too.

**Reflect:** Does every guru syllable receive a high pitch?

**Explanation:** No. Weight and Vedic accent are different dimensions. Follow the transmitted accents and the recitation convention of the source.

### 40. Variants, pāṭhas and the limits of a simple count

**Outcome:** Understand why received text and metrical analysis may differ.

Vedic study distinguishes continuous saṃhitā recitation, word-separated pada recitation and other organized recitation methods. These preserve and teach the text in different ways. They are not different metres produced by moving a swipe control.

**Cases and examples**

- **Saṃhitā-pāṭha and pada-pāṭha** — The continuous form includes connections between words; the word-separated form helps establish their identity. Scan the appropriate form for the question you are asking.
- **Krama and jaṭā** — Krama links consecutive words in pairs. Jaṭā uses a more elaborate forward-and-back arrangement. Their repetitions serve transmission, not a new stanza count.
- **Nicṛt and bhurij** — Piṅgala distinguishes forms deficient or excessive by one syllable. A short count can be a recognized classification, not proof of a copying error.

**Go deeper**

Vedic metre may also require examining historical syllabification or a resolution supported by the text’s scholarly treatment. Do not silently expand a sound or remove a sandhi to achieve a preferred total. Record the received form, the metrical interpretation and the authority for it separately. Full expertise requires work within particular texts and traditions, beyond this survey.

**Reflect:** A Vedic stanza is one syllable short of the standard shape. What should happen next?

**Explanation:** Check the text, its assigned metre and the tradition’s analysis, including a possible nicṛt form. Do not automatically edit it.

### 41. Read a metrical definition in Sanskrit

**Outcome:** Turn a compact rule into a usable pattern.

Traditional manuals often encode a metre’s prescription in a verse that also demonstrates the metre. Read the technical vocabulary first, expand the gaṇas, count the positions and identify any numerical words specifying divisions.

**Cases and examples**

- **bhujaṅgaprayātaṃ bhaved yaiś caturbhiḥ** — Four ya-gaṇas: LGG repeated four times. The definition both names and exemplifies Bhujaṅgaprayāta.
- **Mandākrāntā: ma · bha · na · ta · ta · G · G** — Expand to GGG GLL LLL GGL GGL GG, then regroup as 4 + 6 + 7 to inspect the yati.
- **Number words in definitions** — Words such as abdhi (ocean: four in this convention) and rasa (six) can encode counts. Their function is numerical rather than the literal imagery of the example.

**Go deeper**

Keep the definition, your expansion and a literary example distinct. A digital edition may report a variant where the wording no longer matches its apparent pattern. That is a reason to consult the apparatus, not to copy the inconsistent string into a teaching app. The source-linked method lets a learner examine unfamiliar metres beyond this course.

**Reflect:** Why can a defining verse be especially useful?

**Explanation:** It gives a verbal rule and an example to scan. Agreement between the two is a check; disagreement calls for investigation of the text or interpretation.

### 42. Piṅgala’s combinatorial world

**Outcome:** Generate and count possible patterns systematically.

For a fixed number of syllables, each position can be L or G. Three positions produce eight patterns. This systematic expansion is prastāra. It explains why the eight three-syllable gaṇas cover every possible arrangement.

**Cases and examples**

- **Three-position expansion** — GGG, LGG, GLG, LLG, GGL, LGL, GLL, LLL. This ordering changes the leftmost position fastest.
- **Saṃkhyā: how many?** — For n independently chosen positions, there are 2ⁿ patterns. Four positions give sixteen. Only some patterns become established poetic metres.
- **Uddiṣṭa and naṣṭa** — In the displayed one-based ordering, LGL is row 6. Uddiṣṭa finds a pattern’s row; naṣṭa recovers the pattern from a row number.

**Go deeper**

Using G=0 and L=1 as a modern explanation, the left-to-right place values here are 1, 2, 4. LGL gives 1+4, then add 1 for row 6. Ekadvyādilagakriyā counts patterns with specified numbers of light syllables: for three positions the counts are 1, 3, 3, 1. Adhvayoga concerns the extent of the arranged expansion. These are analytical procedures, not instructions to perform every possible pattern as poetry.

**Reflect:** How many three-syllable patterns contain exactly one L?

**Explanation:** Three: LGG, GLG and GGL. Choosing the light position determines the pattern.

Sources: [Vṛttaratnākara · Enumeration](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/Textual-Studies/Vrttaratnakara/6-Prastharadi.htm)

### 43. When a tool disagrees with the poem

**Outcome:** Use computational results as evidence.

A metre recognizer can compare a scan against many definitions, but its output depends on the text, transcription, segmentation and supported rules. A close match should lead you to the differing positions. It should not silently rewrite the poem.

**Cases and examples**

- **One unexpected syllable** — Check for a missing vowel sign, an accidental extra vowel, duplicated text or the wrong quarter boundary.
- **One unexpected weight** — Check long vowels, clusters, anusvāra, visarga, connected-word context and ending treatment before invoking an exception.
- **Several possible matches** — Compare all pādas, yati and the source’s classification. Report the alternatives rather than pretending the name is certain.

**Go deeper**

Keep an audit trail: source reading → syllable division → weight reasons → candidate metre → remaining mismatch. OCR and transliteration can create apparent metrical faults. A manuscript variant can help explain a mismatch but does not prove which reading is correct. Meaning, grammar and textual evidence must participate in that decision.

**Reflect:** A tool offers to delete a syllable for a perfect match. Should the lesson accept it automatically?

**Explanation:** No. Preserve the source text, identify the discrepancy and look for textual evidence before proposing a correction.

### 44. Compose with constraints and meaning

**Outcome:** Use a pattern as a scaffold without sacrificing Sanskrit.

Composition joins several skills: a coherent thought, grammatical words, correct sandhi and a metrical structure. Begin with one pāda in a familiar pattern. Treat it as a draft; metre is one requirement, not a substitute for syntax or sense.

**Cases and examples**

- **Choose a target** — For an ordinary odd śloka pāda, plan eight syllables and the LGGX cadence, then check the opening restrictions.
- **Write the thought in prose first** — Choose a small idea you can express accurately. Identify the case relations and verb before reshaping the wording.
- **Revise one variable at a time** — A synonym can change length; word order changes neighbouring sounds; sandhi can change the vowel count. Re-scan after each revision.

**Go deeper**

Do not shorten a long vowel arbitrarily or insert meaningless particles to obtain a match. A good review asks: Is the statement understandable? Is the grammar defensible? Is the connected text correct? Does every weight have a reason? Is the chosen metre genuinely satisfied? Keep the initial prose and revised verse together so the learner can explain the choices.

**Reflect:** A line fits perfectly but has the wrong noun case. Is it finished?

**Explanation:** No. Metrical correctness does not repair grammar or meaning. Revise and scan again.

### 45. Studio: speech and meaning in Raghuvaṃśa

**Outcome:** Read a fresh complete śloka with a worked explanation.

Kālidāsa opens Raghuvaṃśa by saluting Pārvatī and Parameśvara, joined like speech and meaning, for understanding speech and meaning. First read the complete Sanskrit in the examples. Then check its four eight-syllable pādas without referring to the Gītā solution.

**Cases and examples**

- **वागर्थाविव सम्पृक्तौ · G G G L | L G G G** — vā-ga-rthā-vi-va-saṃ-pṛ-ktau. Ga is heavy before r+th; pṛ is heavy before k+t. The odd cadence is regular.
- **वागर्थप्रतिपत्तये · G G G L | L G L G** — vā-ga-rtha-pra-ti-pa-tta-ye. Rtha is heavy before p+r. Pa is heavy before t+t. The even cadence is regular.
- **जगतः पितरौ वन्दे · L L G L | L G G G** — ja-ga-taḥ-pi-ta-rau-va-nde. Taḥ is heavy from visarga, rau from au, and va from n+d.
- **पार्वतीपरमेश्वरौ · G L G L | L G L G** — pā-rva-tī-pa-ra-me-śva-rau. Long ā, ī, e and au supply the heavy positions shown.

**Go deeper**

Notice three different reasons for G within one short stanza: a long vowel, a following cluster and visarga or anusvāra. The grammar also matters: pitarau is a dual form, “the two parents,” referring to the pair named in the fourth pāda. Read the four quarters again as one sentence after finishing the technical scan.

**Reflect:** Why is rtha in vāgarthapratipattaye heavy although its vowel a is short?

**Explanation:** Because p+r follows it before the next vowel. The cluster at the start of prati affects the preceding syllable.

### 46. Studio: the opening of Meghadūta

**Outcome:** Navigate a whole long stanza and its textual choices.

The opening introduces a yakṣa, punished by his lord after neglecting his duty, living apart from his beloved at Rāmagiri. Read the four Mandākrāntā pādas below. Each has seventeen syllables organized 4 + 6 + 7; preserve the source reading throughout.

**Cases and examples**

- **कश्चित् कान्ताविरहगुरुणा स्वाधिकारप्रमत्तः** — kaścit kāntā | virahaguruṇā | svādhikārapramattaḥ. Four heavy syllables begin the line. The first division falls between members of the compound.
- **शापेनास्तङ्गमितमहिमा वर्षभोग्येण भर्तुः** — śāpenāstaṃ | gamitamahimā | varṣabhogyeṇa bhartuḥ. The curse has diminished his greatness; it is to be endured for a year.
- **यक्षश्चक्रे जनकतनयास्नानपुण्योदकेषु** — yakṣaś cakre | janakatanayā | snānapuṇyodakeṣu. The line describes waters made holy by the bathing of Janaka’s daughter.
- **स्निग्धच्छायातरुषु वसतिं रामगिर्याश्रमेषु** — snigdhacchāyā | taruṣu vasatiṃ | rāmagiryāśrameṣu. He makes his dwelling in Rāmagiri’s hermitages, among trees with welcoming shade.

**Go deeper**

The working pattern is GGGG | LLLLLG | GLGGLGG, with ending treatment checked against usage. Final ṣu in pādas 3 and 4 is naturally light; mark the metrical allowance explicitly. Another reading has svādhikārāt pramattaḥ in pāda 1, with a different consonant environment but the same overall pattern. Record the chosen reading rather than calling one wrong from metre alone.

**Reflect:** Why should a lesson show the selected reading alongside its source?

**Explanation:** Readers may encounter a different edition. Seeing the reading and its scan prevents normal textual variation from looking like an unexplained error.

### 47. Studio: explain Āryā to another learner

**Outcome:** Show the internal structure instead of reciting totals.

Imagine teaching someone who knows only “12, 18, 12, 15.” Show what that summary leaves out using Vṛttaratnākara 2.23. The verse describes Āryā while demonstrating it: seven groups plus a heavy ending, no ja in odd groups, and a specially regulated sixth group.

**Cases and examples**

- **lakṣmaitat sapta gaṇā gopetā bhavati neha viṣame jaḥ** — First half of the Sanskrit definition. Meaning in outline: these are its characteristics—seven groups, with a final heavy; no ja-group in an odd position.
- **lakṣ mai | tat sap | ta ga ṇā || go pe | tā bha va | ti ne ha | vi ṣa me | jaḥ** — GG | GG | LLG || GG | GLL | LGL | LLG | G. The first three groups give 12; the rest give 18. The sixth group ti-ne-ha is LGL. Dividers show metrical groups, not word separation.
- **ṣaṣṭho ’yaṃ nalaghū vā prathame ’rdhe niyatam āryāyāḥ** — Second half continues the regulation of the sixth group in the first half. The expression na + laghu gives four lights as its alternative. The next rule, 2.24, supplies further division conditions and the second-half treatment.
- **ṣaṣ ṭho | yaṃ na la | ghū vā || pra tha me | rdhe ni ya | ta | mā ryā | yāḥ** — GG | GLL | GG || LLG | GLL | L | GG | G. Read 4+4+4 || 4+4+1+4+2 = 12 || 15. Sandhi gives niyatamāryāyāḥ across the last groups.

**Go deeper**

Now try replacing the first GG group with LGL. It still totals four, but the odd-group restriction rejects it. This near-miss shows why a complete explanation needs both arithmetic and position. The real Sanskrit example also shows why mātrā-group divisions cannot be mistaken for grammatical word boundaries; keep the unbroken verse beside the analysis.

**Reflect:** What is the best explanation of why the proposed first-group swap fails?

**Explanation:** The total is unchanged, but LGL is excluded in Āryā’s odd full groups. A correct total cannot override a positional rule.

Sources: [Vṛttaratnākara · Āryā](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/Textual-Studies/Vrttaratnakara/2-Matravrtta-01.htm)

### 48. Finish with evidence, then choose a branch

**Outcome:** Show what you can transfer to a new verse.

A completed reading should contain the text and source, pāda division, vowel count, weight reasons, candidate metre, relevant exceptions and a plain-language meaning. Then read it fluently. This is a stronger endpoint than remembering a list of metre names.

**Cases and examples**

- **Classical reader · continue from studios 1 and 2** — Take a new sourced śloka, Upajāti and long-metre stanza. Produce the same annotated reading, then compare with an edition or teacher.
- **Composer · continue from the composition lesson** — Write a small complete stanza, keep the prose sense, and obtain grammatical as well as metrical review.
- **Vedic learner · continue within a named tradition** — Study matched accented texts and reviewed recordings with a teacher. Extend metre, phonetics and recitation together.
- **Scholar · continue through the primary manuals** — Use the catalogue for rare metres, commentaries for exceptions and multiple editions for textual problems. Retain uncertainty when the evidence is unresolved.

**Go deeper**

This course covers the core mechanisms, common Classical repertoire, mātrā families, Vedic orientation and the tools for further reading. It does not exhaust every historical metre, every commentary, every Vedic śākhā or the independent prosodies of Pāli, Prakrit and regional languages. Breadth means a complete navigational map with honest depth labels—not an unsupported claim that 48 lessons replace the whole discipline.

**Reflect:** What would demonstrate understanding on a verse you have never seen?

**Explanation:** An independently justified scan, a defensible classification, an account of any uncertainty, and a coherent reading of the meaning. The lesson interface can record review, but cannot certify these abilities from a button press.
