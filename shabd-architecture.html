Sanskritam · System Design

Architecture

How the pieces fit — organised around cost and register rather than features, because the marginal cost of each surface differs by two orders of magnitude and the two registers of Sanskrit need different voices entirely.

Updated 29 August 2026 React Native · Expo Sanskrit first

The organising principle

Feature lists make bad architectures here. Two facts should decide where code lives: surfaces differ enormously in what they cost to serve, and the two registers of Sanskrit require different models end to end.

Cost tiers
TierMarginal costSurfacesStrategy
Pre-computed~₹0 Audio challenges, daily śloka, grammar Render once offline, serve to everyone from CDN
Cheap live₹0.5–2 Recognition, tutor reasoning, diagnosis Call per request; no metering needed
Expensive live₹2.5–18 Streaming speech for open conversation Meter it, gate it behind the paid tier

Everything that follows is an attempt to push as much as possible into the first row.

The system

The piece most people leave out

The offline pipeline is where quality comes from. Nothing in it is latency-sensitive, so it can afford slow, validated generation with a human in the loop — and every artifact it produces is free to serve forever. Build it before the app, not after.

Register is a routing key

Sanskrit is not one language for our purposes. Classical and Vedic verse is chanted; laukika is spoken. They need different corpora, different voices, and different difficulty orderings — so register selects all three, explicitly, at the routing layer.

Surface → register → stack

Enforce the corpus split at the retrieval layer, not by prompt instruction. A shared index makes the tutor answer "what did you eat?" in Purāṇic register — which is not a subtle degradation, it is the product sounding absurd.

Su-śrotā publishes no licence file. Under default copyright that means all rights reserved, not free use — open-sourced in spirit is not licensed in law. Grading

Compare parses, never strings

Sanskrit has free word order. The same sentence is correct in several arrangements, so string comparison marks right answers wrong — and a learner told they are wrong when they are right will quit.

Align against the target, don't transcribe freely

Saaras is trained on correct Sanskrit. A learner's mangled attempt can transcribe as the valid form, silently erasing the very error the app exists to catch.

For challenges and śloka recitation we always know the target text — so verify akṣara by akṣara against it rather than transcribing open-vocabulary. The failure mode disappears entirely. This is the Vāgbodhinī approach, and it is correct regardless of whose code ends up implementing it.

Product principle · abstain rather than accuse

Flag a syllable only when multiple decodes agree; stay silent when uncertain. A missed error costs far less than a false accusation. A learner wrongly told they mispronounced something loses trust immediately and permanently — and in Sanskrit, where pronunciation carries real weight, that trust does not come back.

Data model

The grammatical feature taxonomy is the spine. Challenge selection, the ratio dial, and the diagnosis text all hang off it.

Feature            id, kind (case|number|tense|voice|sandhi), rank
                   e.g. ACC.SG · DUAL · PRES.1SG · vowel-sandhi

Challenge          id, prompt_l1, prompt_lang, target_sanskrit,
                   features[]        → what it exercises
                   accepted_parses[] → feature sets, NOT strings
                   audio_url         → pre-rendered, CDN
                   register          → laukika | classical

Shloka             id, date, source_ref, devanagari, iast, metre,
                   word_breakdown[], features[], audio_url

Learner            id, bridge_langs[], sanskrit_ratio, script_ratio,
                   feature_mastery{feature_id → score},
                   streak, voice_minutes_used

Attempt            learner_id, challenge_id, raw_audio, alignment[],
                   parsed_features[], diff[], verdict, abstained[], ts

Two independent ratios. sanskrit_ratio governs how much target language appears; script_ratio governs how much Devanagari replaces IAST. They move separately because the two markets need opposite settings — a diaspora beginner may handle Sanskrit words but cannot read the script; a Hindi speaker reads Devanagari natively and needs the language faded in slowly.

Feature mastery is what the dial reads. Raise the Sanskrit ratio as mastery broadens, and select the next challenge at the edge of what is known rather than by a global difficulty number. Vidyut can detect which features a sentence requires, so the whole corpus auto-tags.

One ordering note: the dual number (द्विवचन) is alien to English and Hindi speakers and appears constantly. Give it its own slot in the progression rather than folding it into plural.

Relationship to the IISc Sanskrit stack

Prof. Prathosh A P's work covers chanting and śāstric recitation. This product covers conversational production. They are complements, not substitutes — which is why two of his components slot in cleanly and none of them compete for the same job.

Overlap
HisOurs
Vāgdhenu — chant TTSAdopted directly for śloka audio
Su-śrotā — recitation ASRSaaras codemix for conversation; Su-śrotā blocked on licence
Vāgbodhinī — chant practice against a known verseAdaptive code-switching tutor, laukika production

Vāgbodhinī partially overlaps the daily-śloka feature and proves that syllable-level pronunciation feedback works. It does not do conversation, code-switching, an adaptive ratio, or laukika Sanskrit — so the lane stays open.

Worth approaching him directly regardless of the licence question: he open-sources actively, and he is a strong candidate for the expert content validation this product needs anyway.

One caution on sourcing — claims circulating on social media about an "automated Śikṣā and Chandas verification engine" and a large contributor count overstate what is documented. What exists is metre-aware rendering and akṣara-level verification against a known target. Build against the repos, not the commentary.

Build order

Each step de-risks the next. Nothing here is thrown away.

  1. Voice spike — both registers at onceVāgdhenu renders 20 śloka, Indic Parler-TTS renders 20 laukika sentences, Vidyut validates both. Send the audio to three Sanskrit speakers. Closes the largest unknown and validates the register split in a single pass.
  2. Recognition spikeRecord genuinely code-mixed Hindi–English–Sanskrit speech and run it through Saaras codemix. Confirm it neither collapses nor silently repairs learner errors.
  3. Content pipelineCorpora in, validated pre-rendered challenges out, forked by register. Everything downstream depends on it, and it produces the asset that makes the free tier cost nothing.
  4. API and learner modelAuth, entitlements, feature taxonomy, selection engine, register routing. Server-side so the algorithm can be tuned without shipping releases.
  5. Expo client — challenges onlyThe habit loop, offline-capable, served entirely from the CDN. Ship this and get real users before building conversation.
  6. Daily ślokaSame pipeline, one item a day, Vāgdhenu voice. Acquisition and retention, and it accretes into a permanent archive over time.
  7. Conversation, meteredThe premium tier, added once people are paying for the free loop and the voice quality is proven.
