01 — What changed since August

Two facts arrived after the market doc was written

Both of them move the plan. Neither is in sanskrit-market.html, which was compiled on 28 August.

Sanskrit stopped being optional for a large cohort of Indian schoolchildren

CBSE issued a directive on 10 April 2026, with a one-week compliance window, making a third language and two Indian languages compulsory from Class 6 through Class 10, rolling out with Class 6 in 2026. Because English-medium schools classify English as the foreign language, Sanskrit becomes the default third option — and French, German and Spanish are being discontinued to make room. NCF-aligned textbooks are not ready; schools were told to use locally available material. A Supreme Court challenge is live.

Three things follow. First, this is involuntary demand with a deadline, and it converts differently from cultural enthusiasm: the parent buys, the child uses, and the decision is made under exam pressure. That is the segment that actually pays in India. Second, the curriculum gap is your gap — no textbook, and teachers themselves largely untrained in Sanskrit. Third, and worth naming plainly: Sanskrit was, for a generation, the single most-hated subject in school. You are not entering a market of enthusiasts. You are entering a market of resentment, which is a better market, because the incumbent experience is rote memorisation and nobody has tried to fix it.

Do not bet the company on the policy. It is in flux and in litigation. But the feature it implies — declension and conjugation mastery with a derivation behind every cell — is one you get nearly free from P9. Build it because the engine hands it to you, not because the rule holds.

The category leader made "AI-generated" a liability

Duolingo went AI-first in April 2025. Users called the content slop, cancelled publicly, and broke long streaks in protest; the stock is reported down roughly 80% from its May 2025 peak as of April 2026. Duolingo also shipped an Energy system that meters free users per exercise regardless of whether they answer correctly — friction its own learners resent. Airlearn, the nimble challenger, leans heavily on AI-generated assets too.

Every competitor's "why was I wrong?" a guess A language model explaining grammar Plausible, fluent, unfalsifiable, and wrong often enough that your own DECISIONS log records ~50% false accusations on dual-bearing content.
What Sanskritam can return instead a proof A Pāṇinian derivation with rule numbers Deterministic, offline, ₹0 per call, and checkable by anyone holding a copy of the Aṣṭādhyāyī.
The position that just opened

Verified, not generated. Sanskrit is the one major language where a machine can check grammar deterministically, and you already run that check — the P6-3 gate measures an 83.3% pass rate. Make it visible: a mark on content that passed the gate, and a drawer that shows the derivation. This is not garnish. It is the single claim your architecture earns and your competitors' cannot, and the market has just made it valuable.

04 — Games

Nine modes, ranked by what they return

Each of these is a game a Sanskrit app can have and a French app structurally cannot. Chips mark whether the engine is already on disk and roughly what the build costs.

वाक्य-क्रीडा The sentence that will not break

Four word-tiles. Drag them into any order — the sentence keeps its meaning, every time. Then change one ending, and it breaks. That single interaction teaches vibhakti better than a term of explanation, because the learner discovers that meaning lives in the endings by trying to destroy it and failing.

Also your best advertisement. It is a fifteen-second video that explains why Sanskrit is not English, and nobody has made it.

engine: shippedbuild: S
सन्धि-भेदः The collision

Two words slide toward each other; the learner predicts the junction before it lands. Reversed: a joined form arrives and must be split. Marked deterministically, with the rule named underneath.

U4-5 already specifies the animation. Making it a game costs little more — and sandhi is the concept learners actually quit over, so the highest-value place to spend interaction design is here.

engine: idle, on diskbuild: M
पाणिनि-यन्त्रम् Pāṇini's machine

You are given भू + लट् + prathama + eka and a rack of sūtras. Apply them in the right order to reach भवति. A wrong rule doesn't buzz — it shows you the form it would have produced, which is the entire lesson.

This is a Zachtronics-style optimisation puzzle that happens to be a 2,500-year-old grammar. It is also the single most postable thing in the project — see the programmer wedge in §6.

engine: idle, on diskbuild: M₹0 / play
छन्दः-क्रीडा The rhythm game

The verse recites; you tap light and heavy in time. Miss a weight and the metre stumbles audibly. It uses the Bulbul audio you are already paying for, and the note chart is computed, so every verse in Sanskrit literature is a level you did not author.

It also converts śloka memorisation into a music game — which is close to how memorisation actually worked for three millennia.

engine: idle, on diskbuild: M
शब्दरूप-द्वन्द्वः The table, defused

The declension grid as a timed board, spaced repetition on the cells you miss, and a why on every cell that opens the derivation. Students are told to memorise these because their teachers have no way to show the pattern. You can show the pattern.

This is the most-hated, most-examined object in school Sanskrit, and it aims straight at the cohort described in §1.

engine: idle, on diskbuild: Msegment: CBSE
वर्ण-बन्धः The daily word

Wordle, but scored on the varṇamālā grid: क against ग is one step — same place of articulation, different voicing — and gets its own colour, while क against प is far. "Nearly right" finally has a principled meaning, which a Latin-alphabet Wordle can never have, because English letter order is arbitrary and Sanskrit's is a map of the mouth.

Hindi has Shabdle. Sanskrit has nobody. One puzzle a day, no account, web-first — the best organic acquisition loop available to you.

engine: shippedbuild: Sgrowth loop
श्लोक-अन्त्याक्षरी The chain

Antākṣarī, played with verses: the next player must begin on the syllable the last one ended on. Asynchronous, playable over WhatsApp, family against family.

The rules need no teaching — every Indian family already knows them. It is also the only multiplayer mode a grandparent in Pune and a grandchild in New Jersey will both actually open.

engine: idle + open corpusbuild: Mdiaspora
अवधानम् The boss mode

The real art form, made playable: hold a line of verse, count the bell, restore a scrambled stanza, and ignore the heckler — the traditional apraṣṭuta-prasaṅgī, whose actual historical job is to break your concentration. Score is how many streams you held.

You are not inventing a leaderboard. Aṣṭāvadhānī (eight streams) and śatāvadhānī (a hundred) are contested titles that living performers hold. This is the mode that gets written about.

build: Lbrand-defining
कटपयादि The cipher

Numbers written as words and words read as numbers. Decode a verse into a date; encode your own phone number into a word that means something.

Already scoped as P10-2. Worth pulling forward only because ciphers are inherently shareable, and this one is a thousand years old.

05 — Maths

The best maths hook is not Līlāvatī

P10 already exists and its discipline is right: audit numeral coverage before authoring a single problem, and stop the phase if confident mis-parses are common. Two amendments.

Lead with Piṅgala, not Bhāskara. The Chandaḥśāstra (c. 3rd–2nd century BCE) describes prosody in a system that is genuinely binary — light and heavy are two states, enumerated exhaustively — and contains the meru-prastāra, the triangle Europe would later name for Pascal, made explicit in Halāyudha's 10th-century commentary. Its mātrāmeru sequence is the Fibonacci sequence, centuries before Fibonacci.

Why that beats Līlāvatī as the opening: a learner who has played the rhythm game has already been doing binary arithmetic for a week without being told. Revealing it is an earned discovery rather than a claim on a marketing page — and it costs nothing extra, because it rides on a game you were building anyway. Līlāvatī's bees and peacocks are lovely and public domain; keep them as the second beat, not the first.

Do not ship

"Vedic Mathematics" is not ancient. The sixteen-sūtra system is Bharati Krishna Tirtha's, published in 1965, and its claimed Vedic provenance has never been substantiated. The tricks are fine; the label is false. Using it would trade your one durable asset — being the app that does not make things up — for a marketing sentence you do not need, in a category where fact-checkers are already active.

06 — Acquisition

How to actually fetch people

The 28 August analysis settled the strategy: paid acquisition is arithmetically fatal at roughly ₹46 of lifetime revenue per download, and the channels are creators, temple schools and Samskrita Bharati. That still holds. What follows is the mechanics — what makes each channel compound instead of spend.

  1. Ship the toys before the app Three free web pages, no account, no install: the derivation viewer ("type any word, watch Pāṇini derive it"), the metre scanner ("paste a verse, see its rhythm"), and the daily word. All three run on engines already on disk, offline, at ₹0 marginal cost — so they can be uncapped and free forever, which is exactly what makes them spread. One weekend each.
  2. The programmer wedge, which nobody has taken Pāṇini's grammar is a formal system that predates Backus–Naur form by some 2,500 years — Ingerman proposed in 1967 that BNF be renamed Pāṇini–Backus Form, and the Pāṇinian formalism is strictly more expressive than context-free grammars. A working derivation viewer is a front-page post on Hacker News and r/programming, and that audience contains precisely the people who install a Sanskrit app for reasons other than devotion. It has never been shipped as a usable web tool.
  3. Supply the creators; don't sponsor them The ecosystem is real: The Sanskrit Channel (~1.3M subscribers), Vyoma, Sanskritshree, the sanskritsparrow walking groups in Cubbon Park and the ~1,000-member Kimbho WhatsApp group, Yash Salunke's Gen Z Sanskrit comedy. They share one problem — explaining grammar on camera is hard and they have no visuals. Give them the derivation ladder and the scansion strip as free embeddable widgets carrying a small mark. Sponsorship buys one video; being the tool inside every video compounds.
  4. Go where the deadline is The CBSE cohort in §1 has a date, no textbook, and untrained teachers. A free teacher pack — the śabdarūpa and dhāturūpa the syllabus actually tests, each with its derivation, printable — costs a weekend of content work and is the only realistic route into the institutional licensing band that the August model needs for its upside case.
  5. Build every share surface WhatsApp-shaped India's sharing layer is not email and not a link preview. It is an image, one line of text, and a URL, forwarded into a family group. Design the daily-word result card, the antākṣarī challenge and the family streak as that object from the start, rather than retrofitting sharing later.
  6. "You already know 300 Sanskrit words" Carried over from August, and still the best cold open available: it is true, it is shareable, and it doubles as onboarding — start the learner from words they already own instead of from the alphabet. Most Sanskrit courses begin by making the learner feel illiterate. This one begins by telling them they are already halfway in.
  7. Duolingo's refusal now has a second act They declined to build Sanskrit citing low demand, with a petition still running. They then shipped AI content their own users publicly called slop. "The app Duolingo wouldn't build, built the way Duolingo stopped building" is a headline, an antagonist and a demand proof in one line — and unlike most positioning, you can substantiate it with the gate's pass rate.
07 — Interface

Where to spend the motion budget

UI_HANDOVER.md already covers the mascot, the path, the verdict moment and the articulation map, and its ranking is sound. Five additions that are specific to this language rather than to this category.

Animate the śirorekhā — the signature motion nobody else can have

Devanagari hangs from a headline. So a word can be built the way it is written: the horizontal rule draws left to right, and the glyphs descend from it into place. Latin type has no equivalent gesture — there is nothing to hang from. Used once, on the word-reveal moment, it becomes the app's visual signature; used everywhere it becomes noise. Spend it in one place.

Order the sound design the way the alphabet is ordered

The varṇamālā runs from the throat to the lips. The interface sounds can run the same way: back-of-mouth rows get darker, lower feedback tones; labial rows get brighter, forward ones. A learner who never reads the explanation still absorbs the axis through their ears over a few hundred sessions. This is free — it is a choice about five sound files, not a feature.

Make the proof drawer the anti-slop moment, visibly

Duolingo's "Explain My Answer" is a model talking, and that is all it is. Yours says what the sentence means in plain language — that part is still the model, and still the right tool for it — and then opens a drawer with numbered rules underneath, where the grammatical claim gets proved rather than asserted. Give the drawer a physical metaphor with restraint — an unrolling, not a temple — and put the rule count in the header, because 20 steps is itself the claim. This is the one screen worth showing in every piece of marketing you ever make.

Keep abstention as a character trait, and say so out loud

The unsure violet is defined in theme.ts and still unused. A tutor that says "I couldn't hear that clearly — no penalty" instead of guessing is doing something no competitor does, and it is the emotional counterpart to the verified-not-generated position. It belongs in the store listing, not just the code.

Do not add hearts, energy, or any meter that stops a willing learner

Duolingo is actively losing goodwill over exactly this, right now. A Sanskrit learner who wants a sixth lesson is the rarest thing you have. Never charge them friction for it; find the money in the diaspora subscription and the institutional pack instead.

08 — Correction

Two claims to drop, one to make louder

You framed this as "the most ancient language, and very scientific." One of those is weaker than it sounds, one is a documented myth, and one is far stronger than the version usually told. This is a product decision, not a pedantic one: the whole differentiator is being the app that does not make things up, and you cannot sell verified content on debunked marketing.

"The oldest language" — don't claim it

Sumerian and Egyptian have written records older by well over a millennium. The true claim is more interesting anyway: Sanskrit is among the oldest continuously used literary languages on earth, preserved through an oral transmission of such fidelity that the preservation techniques are themselves worth teaching — and would make a genuinely good lesson.

"NASA said Sanskrit is the best language for computers" — this is a myth

It traces to Rick Briggs' 1985 paper in AI Magazine, written at RIACS, NASA Ames. What the paper actually argued is better than the myth: that Sanskrit grammarians had already built, for a natural language, the unambiguous agent–action–object representations that 1980s AI was calling semantic networks — that the field had reinvented a wheel. There is no supercomputer programme, no sixth-generation plan, and no claim that Sanskrit is a programming language. Indian fact-checkers have run at this claim repeatedly. Cite the real paper; it is a better story and it survives scrutiny.

"Scientific" — say the precise version, which is extraordinary

Pāṇini's Aṣṭādhyāyī (c. 5th–4th century BCE) is the earliest known formal generative grammar: roughly 4,000 sūtras with metarules, ordered application and recursion, sufficient to derive the language's forms. Ingerman's 1967 proposal to rename BNF "Pāṇini–Backus Form" is real. The Pāṇinian formalism is strictly more powerful than the context-free grammars BNF describes.

The point

You do not need the myth, because you are running the actual thing. The twenty-step derivation in §2 is not an argument that Sanskrit is scientific. It is the science, executing, on your machine, offline, for nothing. Everything in this document is downstream of that one fact.

09 — Sequence

What I would actually do next

Constraint first: you are one person, and HANDOVER.md is right that content at volume (P7) is the bottleneck. Nothing here should displace it. But the first five items cost days rather than weeks, because the engine is already on disk and the marginal cost of running it is zero.
