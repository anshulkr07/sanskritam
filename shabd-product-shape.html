What the app actually is

Information architecture, session anatomy, and the visual direction — organised around what a learner opens the app to do, not around what kinds of content exist.

The reorganisation

A feature list organised by content type produces a six-tab nav where the user faces a menu and picks nothing. Two of the six proposed surfaces are not destinations at all — they are layers that belong inside the moment of learning.

The varṇamālā is a grid, not an alphabet

Devanagari's order is not arbitrary like A-B-C. The consonants form a 5×5 matrix organised by articulation — row is where the tongue goes, column is voicing and aspiration. Position in the grid is the pronunciation instruction.

Labial · ओष्ठ्यपफबभम

So the page is not a scrolling list of 33 shapes to memorise. Tap a cell and get three things at once: the sound, a mouth-position diagram, and a stroke-order animation. Move down a column and hear the place of articulation shift; move across a row and hear voicing switch on. The learner discovers a system rather than memorising glyphs — which is both faster and the first moment of awe.

It also gives the minimal-pair drill a natural home. The confusions that matter — श/ष/स for Hindi speakers, ट vs त for English speakers — are adjacent cells. The grid explains why they are confusable, which is most of the fix.

Stroke animation is cheap: an SVG path animated with stroke-dasharray. No video, no library, works offline, scales to any size.

Progressive disclosure

Pin the varṇamālā to the top of Explore until the learner has covered the alphabet, then let it recede. It is used heavily for about two weeks and rarely afterwards — but in those two weeks it is the most important screen in the product, particularly for diaspora learners who arrive unable to read the script at all.

Grammar as a live rule engine

Sanskrit grammar is deterministic, formally specified, and genuinely startling to anyone with a technical background — Pāṇini's Aṣṭādhyāyī is a generative rule system with meta-rules governing rule application, written some two and a half millennia before anything comparable in computer science.

And you already have the engine. vidyut-prakriyā derives forms from Pāṇinian rules and can expose the derivation steps. It is in the architecture for validation; pointing it at the interface costs almost nothing and produces something no mobile app currently offers.

Four explorable surfaces
SurfaceWhat the learner doesWhy it lands
Prakriyā viewer Pick any form, watch it built step by step, each step labelled with the sūtra that fired Not a textbook table — Pāṇini's system executing, live and verifiable
Sandhi playground Drop two words together, watch the transformation, see the governing rule Sandhi is a deterministic function. Show it as one.
Word-order shuffler One sentence, every valid arrangement, all correct Makes free word order visceral — the case ending carries the role, not the position
Kāraka map Semantic roles mapped onto case endings Effectively a type system. Framing it that way lands hard with technical users.
This is also the marketing engine

Every one of these surfaces is a short video. "Sanskrit had formal generative grammar two and a half thousand years before Backus–Naur form." The content channel that acquisition depends on gets produced by the product itself, at no extra cost — and unlike most app marketing, the claim is true and checkable.

The five-minute session

The session is the product. It should open on a win, work at the edge of what the learner knows, and end on a completion — without ever asking them to choose a mode.

Six features worth adding

01
Tap any word, anywhere
Full morphological breakdown on tap — in a challenge, in the śloka, in a conversation transcript. Vidyut and DCS already give you this, so it costs almost nothing, and it is the most-used feature in every serious language app. Nobody serves it well for Sanskrit on mobile.
Near-free · highest daily use
02
Grammatical coverage as the progress model
Duolingo shows XP, which means nothing. Show "6 of 8 cases · 2 of 3 numbers · 4 of 10 verb classes" — a real map of what has been mastered. More motivating, more honest, and it falls straight out of feature_mastery. Competitors cannot copy this without a morphological analyser.
Differentiator · free from existing data
03
Chant-along with syllable highlighting
Vāgdhenu renders the verse, forced alignment scores the learner chanting it back, and each akṣara lights as it passes. Karaoke for śloka. Architecturally almost free given what is already planned — and the most shareable thing in the app.
Shareable · already in the stack
04
Minimal-pair pronunciation drill
Target the sounds the learner's bridge language specifically breaks: retroflex vs dental (ट/त), aspirated vs unaspirated (क/ख), the three sibilants (श/ष/स) that Hindi speakers systematically collapse. Drills chosen by which language you already speak — and each pair is a pair of neighbouring cells in the varṇa grid, so the drill and the explanation are the same object.
Novel · keyed to bridge language
05
Parent / family view
The family tier sells to diaspora parents. They pay, the child uses it, and they need to see progress to keep paying. Without this the tier does not convert — which makes it monetisation-critical rather than a nice-to-have.
Monetisation-critical
06
Free talk, corrections off
A place to speak badly with nobody marking it. Correction fatigue is real, and confidence is most of what stops adult learners from opening their mouths. Cheap to build — it is the conversation engine with the correction budget set to zero.
Confidence · trivial to build

One cheap extra: a daily śloka home-screen widget. It shows up every day without the app being opened, costs almost nothing to serve from the CDN, and is the highest-retention-per-effort item on this page.

Visual direction

The trap

The default "Indian cultural app" look — saffron and marigold, temple silhouettes, a Devanagari-flavoured Latin display font, lotus motifs — reads as cheap and is what every generated design produces. Avoid it completely. This is a scholarly product, not a devotional one.

Borrow rubrication, not temples

Sanskrit manuscripts were written in black ink with red reserved for structure — section markers, verse numbers, daṇḍa punctuation. That is a real, specific, non-kitsch visual inheritance: a restrained ground, ink-dark text, and one red used structurally rather than decoratively.

#FBFAF7ground — warm paper, not cream
#1A1A22ink
#A3352Arubric — structure only
#1C6B50correct
#8A8A9Cuncertain — never red

Devanagari is the hero, not an afterthought

Most Indian apps render देवनागरी in the system fallback at body size and it looks cheap. Set it large, in a real face, with generous line-height — the script is beautiful and it can carry the whole design. Pair it with a quiet Latin face rather than competing with it.

Make the dials visible

The ratio dial is the differentiator; do not bury it in settings. Show two sliders — how much Sanskrit, how much Devanagari — and let the learner move them. They are independent because the markets are opposite: a diaspora beginner wants high Sanskrit and low Devanagari, a Hindi speaker wants exactly the reverse.

One rule from the architecture

Uncertain pronunciation feedback must render neutral grey, never red. The abstention principle needs a visual state: "not sure" has to look different from "wrong", or the system quietly becomes the false accuser it was designed not to be.

Six rules to hold the line

RuleWhy it matters here
One obvious action on openA menu of six surfaces gets none of them chosen. Practice is the default tab and today's session is the biggest thing on it.
Teach at the point of errorGrammar and pronunciation reach the learner attached to a mistake, never as a room they must decide to enter.
Never block on audioEvery spoken item needs a typed fallback. The commute is where the daily habit lives, and it is silent.
Abstain rather than accuseA missed error costs far less than telling a learner they were wrong when they were right.
Be honest about latencyConversation will be slow. Show the avatar thinking; never fake instant and never leave dead air unexplained.
Open on a winEvery session starts with something already known. Confidence is the scarce resource in adult language learning.
