01The one finding that matters

Sanskrit speech recognition is solved and free to you. Sanskrit speech synthesis is absent from your funded vendor — but fully available, at higher quality than any other Indic language, from an open-source model under Apache 2.0.

That combination removes the two constraints that looked fatal: the missing voice, and the need to hire a pandit to record one.

Verdict

The flagship language is buildable. Sarvam's Saaras v3 handles Sanskrit input — including native code-mixed speech, which is exactly the input your product receives. Indic Parler-TTS handles Sanskrit output, and rates it the strongest of its 21 languages. Nothing in the pipeline is blocked.

Saaras v3 also covers the rest of the target list — Konkani, Maithili, Sindhi and Odia — so recognition is never the blocker for any planned language.

Verify before building

Sarvam's docs list Odia as od-IN; the website tile says or-IN. Confirm which the API accepts. And since the startup programme gives you a human contact, ask them directly for the Bulbul roadmap on Sanskrit, Konkani, Maithili and Sindhi — that answer is not public anywhere.

03Solving the voice gap

Indic Parler-TTS — from AI4Bharat and HuggingFace, Apache 2.0, self-hostable — officially supports 21 languages, including all four that Sarvam omits.

Its Sanskrit voice does not merely work. Measured by native-speaker score, it is the best-performing language in the entire model — ahead of Tamil, ahead of Hindi.

The one real tradeoff

Parler-TTS is autoregressive, so it is slower than a commercial API. That is a genuine risk for live conversation and a non-issue everywhere else — because the fix is architectural rather than technical.

Architecture

Pre-generate the entire challenge corpus offline and serve it as static files from a CDN. No GPU in the request path, no latency, no marginal cost — the habit loop that drives retention becomes free to serve at any scale. Reserve live synthesis for the premium conversation tier, where it can pay for itself, and keep a Hindi voice as fallback for the bridge-language portions of an utterance.

04Text, grammar and audio

Sanskrit is poorly served commercially and unusually well served academically. The corpora below are the direct consequence of two centuries of philology, and they are better than anything available for most living Indian languages.

Text and grammar

Annotated corpus
Digital Corpus of Sanskrit
Sandhi-split, with full morphological and lexical analysis across all layers and genres of Sanskrit literature. The strongest grammar backbone available for any Indic language.
4,800,000 tagged words · 650,000 lines
Text repository
GRETIL
The Göttingen Register of Electronic Texts in Indian Languages — the standard public-domain repository of machine-readable foundational texts.
Public domain
Validation tooling
Sanskrit Heritage tools
Huet's analysers, used in published work to validate and normalise DCS into a tagged gold corpus. Useful for checking generated Sanskrit programmatically.
Academic, openly available

Speech and audio

TTS training data
IndicVoices-R
The first open-source TTS dataset covering Sanskrit. Comfortably enough to fine-tune if the base Parler voice falls short.
35.75 hrs · 14,604 utterances · 161 speakers
Spoken Sanskrit archive
Samskrita Bharati on Internet Archive
Graded readers, Balamodini children's stories, recorded lectures and Bhagavad Gita classes — read by volunteers, much of it marked public domain.
Free · verify licence per item
Daily spoken source
AIR Sanskrit news
Prasar Bharati broadcasts daily Sanskrit bulletins via newsonair.gov.in — a renewing source of contemporary spoken Sanskrit.
Reuse licence unclear
ASR benchmark
Vedavani
A Sanskrit ASR benchmark corpus. Note the domain: Vedic poetry, not conversational speech — useful for evaluation, misleading as training data for a tutor.
Vedic register only
Two licensing cautions

An uploader named "Samskrita Bharati" does not make every file under it public domain — check the licence on each item you use. Separately, do not voice-clone identifiable speakers from these recordings even where the audio itself is public domain: the recording's copyright status and a person's rights in their own voice are different questions. Use the archives as listening content and ASR training data, not as a source for a synthetic voice.

05What it costs now

Text-to-speech was 75–90% of the variable cost per session. Self-hosting converts it from per-character billing into a fixed GPU line item — which changes which markets are viable.

The domestic Indian market moves from structurally unprofitable to genuinely viable — and the diaspora tier becomes comfortable rather than merely survivable. Pre-generated challenge audio drops the free tier's marginal cost to approximately zero.

Spend the ₹25,000 Sarvam grant on recognition only. At ₹30 per hour that buys roughly 830 hours of user speech — a large beta. Spending any of it on synthesis now buys something you can self-host for less.

06Open questions

In dependency order — each answer determines whether the next question is worth asking.

  1. Generate 20 Sanskrit sentences with the "Aryan" voice A weekend of work that closes the largest remaining unknown. Send the clips to three Sanskrit speakers and ask whether the vowel length and sandhi are right. Their answer decides whether the flagship ships.
  2. Benchmark Parler-TTS latency on a real GPU Determines whether live conversation is viable at launch or has to wait behind the pre-generated challenge bank. Either outcome is workable; the timing of the premium tier depends on it.
  3. Test Saaras v3 codemix mode on genuinely mixed speech Record yourself saying a Hindi–English–Sanskrit sentence of the kind the product is built to receive. Monolingual ASR accuracy tells you nothing about how it handles the real input.
  4. Ask Sarvam for the Bulbul language roadmap One email through the startup programme. If Sanskrit TTS is months away, the self-hosting work becomes a bridge rather than permanent infrastructure — which changes how much to invest in it.
  5. Recruit one Sanskrit expert for content validation No longer needed for recording, still needed for correctness. Reachable remotely through Samskrita Bharati chapters, Central Sanskrit University campuses, Vyoma Labs, the sanskrit-programmers group, or the ashtadhyayi.com community.
